# 124 — Energy Considerations of Large Language Model Inference and Efficiency Optimizations
- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17674 (arXiv; Jared Fernandez, Clara Na, Vashisth Tiwari, Yonatan Bisk, Sasha Luccioni, Emma Strubell; Carnegie Mellon and Hugging Face; v1 24 Apr 2025; 2025-04)
- **Claim**: Measured GPU energy of LLM inference across batch sizes, sequence lengths, decoding strategies, frameworks, GPUs and parallelism. The benefit of each optimization depends on workload shape, software stack and hardware; naive FLOPs-based estimates understate real energy. Applying the right optimizations cuts energy by up to 73% vs vanilla PyTorch.
- **Mechanism**: Energy (NVML via CodeCarbon, GPU only) for 1,024 examples, mean of 3 runs, after warmup (up to 20 batches), bfloat16. Models: mainly Llama-3.1-8B (base and instruct), plus OLMo 1B and 7B, OLMoE 1B-7B, Qwen-1.5-32B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B. Hardware: A6000, A6000 Ada, A100 80GB PCIe (8-GPU nodes, up to 4 GPUs used). Controlled sweeps up to 32K input and 4K output tokens, batch 1 to 1024. A workload model bins each request (input length, output length) to the next-higher power-of-two bin (inputs 32 to 8192, outputs 8 to 512; longer inputs/outputs excluded), batches each bin at the maximum batch size that fits, and sums measured per-batch energy. Compared with an idealized baseline = FLOPs / rated TFLOPS x TDP (A6000: 309.7 TFLOPS, 300 W; FLOPs from the DeepSpeed profiler). Workloads: classical NLP (IMDB, WMT-14, CNN-DailyMail, Wikitext-2, 1,024 samples each) and traces BurstGPT, Azure Chat, Azure Code.
- **Numbers**: Prefill energy is flat at small length and batch (under-saturated GPU) and grows with input length above about 128 tokens; decode dominates energy except for very short outputs (classification, short summaries). Speculative decoding (draft gamma = 4; Llama-3.1-8B with Llama-3.2-1B, and R1-Distill-Qwen-32B with a 1.5B draft) saves energy at batch 16 or less, up to 29.14% vs single-example inference, but needs 25.65% more energy at batch 128. MoE: OLMoE uses up to 54.24% more energy than dense OLMo-1B despite similar active parameters; its fused expert kernel is 19.70% slower at batch 1 and 63% slower at batch 8 than a dense GEMM. Tensor parallelism on Llama-3.1-8B: 2 and 4 GPUs cut latency by 40.16% and 61.34% but raise total energy by 29.3% and 55.23% (batch 1). torch.compile gain: 29.90% on A100, 13.28% on A6000 Ada, 1.96% on A6000; vLLM eager-off gain 25.47% on A100 vs 2.97% on A6000. Online serving with continuous batching saves up to 5% energy at large batch, but costs more at small batch. Table 4, energy above the theoretical ideal (PyTorch vs vLLM): BurstGPT 506.52% vs 63.75%; Azure Code 102.79% vs 26.59%; Azure Conversation 490.23% vs 64.22%. Savings from optimization: 73.00% (BurstGPT), 37.58% (Azure Code), 72.18% (Azure Conversation); the paper says within 26.6% of ideal. Dataset input length stats (mean/median/99th, tokens), as extracted: BurstGPT 256.80/215/1038; Azure Chat 1631.58/928/6683; Azure Code 2511.28/1930/7685. Context cited: Meta says inference is up to 70% of its AI power; Google says 60% of ML energy. No dollar prices.
- **Token-cost implication**: Energy and GPU time per token depend on batch size and output length, not just model size. Self-hosters should use vLLM with continuous batching and CUDA graphs, batch as large as memory allows, and use speculative decoding only at low batch (16 or below). Do not add GPUs via tensor parallelism just to cut latency if cost or energy matters: 2 and 4 GPUs raised energy 29.3% and 55.23%. Prefer dense models over MoE at similar active params if energy per token matters. Cutting output tokens saves more than cutting input since decode dominates. Do not estimate cost from FLOPs and TDP: real energy was up to 6x the ideal in the PyTorch baseline.
- **Caveats**: Independent academic measurement (CMU and Hugging Face), 1B to 32B models, three Ampere/Ada GPUs only, GPU power only (not CPU, memory, disk, cooling). Offline binned simulation, long inputs above 8K and outputs above 512 tokens excluded. Quantization, frequency scaling and disaggregated serving not tested. Energy is not dollars. Samples come from one dataset per run, which the authors say understates the benefit of continuous batching. Some table text is garbled in the extraction, so the Table 1/2 statistics are only partly reliable.
