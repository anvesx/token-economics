                                                                                        A BSTRACT
                                                      LLM agentic workflows increasingly employ different models for specialized
                                                      roles. Users may prefer different trade-offs between task quality and deployment
                                                      cost, sometimes also considering latency. Finding good assignments incurs a sep-
                                                      arate search cost from evaluating multiple candidates. We introduce Cost-Coupled
                                                      Gittins (CC-G ITTINS), a multi-objective extension of single-objective cost-aware
                                                      Gittins search for Pareto recommendation. It exploits the coupling between eval-
                                                      uation and deployment costs: assignments that cost more to deploy also cost more
                                                      to evaluate. The search alternates between seeking higher quality and lower de-
                                                      ployment cost, reusing each evaluation to inform both searches. After each batch,
                                                      CC-G ITTINS recommends assignments that are nondominated under uncertainty-
                                                      adjusted estimates of quality and deployment cost. We evaluate CC-G ITTINS on
                                                      eight quality–cost matrices from workflows with two to five roles and up to 1,681
                                                      candidate assignments. We also evaluate the method with latency as a third objec-
                                                      tive. Across the eight benchmarks, CC-G ITTINS recovers a substantial fraction
                                                      of the true Pareto frontier at roughly 10% of the exhaustive-evaluation cost, and
                                                      recovers most of the frontier with few false positives by 30%.

These roles need not use the same underlying model, and using the most capable models in every
role can incur substantial deployment cost. This creates a model assignment problem—given a set
of candidate models for each role, which assignments best balance end-to-end task quality, deploy-
ment cost, and, when response time matters, latency? Since one role’s output can affect the inputs
and behavior of subsequent roles, an assignment’s quality generally cannot be inferred by evaluating
each model in isolation. The whole workflow must be treated as the unit of evaluation.
Users may want to compare the task quality of inexpensive and more costly assignments before
choosing what to deploy. A representative range of quality–cost trade-offs can support this decision
without requiring every Pareto-optimal assignment to be identified.
Existing methods either report quality–cost frontiers over evaluated agent assignments (TeamBench,
AgentCARD, and AgentOpt) (Kim et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026; Hua et al., 2026), or optimize a
specified objective or constraint. In particular, AgentOpt reduces multi-objective search to single-
objective optimization using a fixed weighted-sum utility (Hua et al., 2026), SCOPE minimizes
deployment cost subject to a quality threshold (Huang et al., 2026), and VineLM adapts model
choices at individual invocations under cost or latency budgets (Pagonas et al., 2026). We study
anytime recommendation of complete role assignments through costly evaluations, without fixing
the preferred trade-off or final search cost in advance.
Exhaustive evaluation is costly: the number of assignments grows multiplicatively across roles, and
each candidate must be run on multiple queries to estimate quality and deployment cost. Figure 1
illustrates quality–cost trade-offs recovered at a fraction of exhaustive evaluation cost.
A key observation is that deployment cost and evaluation cost are naturally coupled. Let Di denote
the mean deployment cost per query for assignment i. Evaluating a batch of B comparable queries
costs approximately κi ≈ BDi . The total search cost accumulates these charges across evaluated
assignments, including those never deployed. Consequently, configurations that are expensive to
deploy are also expensive to learn about.
We introduce Cost-Coupled Gittins (CC-G ITTINS), a multi-objective method for anytime Pareto
recommendation that exploits this coupling. For quality and cost, two search policies, called con-
trollers, take turns choosing evaluations: one seeks higher quality and the other lower deploy-
ment cost. The quality controller prices further evaluation using configuration-specific API costs.
In the simplified model of Section 3.4, its Gittins indices rank assignments by quality minus a
deployment-cost penalty, so relaxing the evaluation-price multiplier can favor different quality–cost
trade-offs. The controllers share observations. After each batch, assignments nondominated un-
der uncertainty-adjusted quality and deployment-cost estimates form the recommendation. Figure 2
gives an overview.
We evaluate CC-G ITTINS through offline replay on eight quality–cost workflow benchmarks, in-
cluding complete HotpotQA and MathQA assignment matrices and six SCOPE-derived instances.
These benchmarks span two to five roles and up to 1,681 candidate assignments. Compared with
five search baselines, CC-G ITTINS achieves competitive hypervolume regret and generational dis-
tance at low search cost, although frontier coverage varies across benchmarks. We further conduct
controlled ablations and evaluate the method with server-reported LLM latency as a third objective
on HotpotQA and MathQA.

1.1     R ELATED W ORK

Resource-aware LLM systems. Server-side systems improve scheduling, memory use, and serv-
ing throughput (Kwon et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2025). Client-side routing selects
a model per request or call (Chen et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2025). Agent configura-
tion search instead assigns models to interacting roles and observes an end-to-end trajectory reward.
TeamBench exhaustively studies the assignments of several providers to Planner, Executor, and Ver-
ifier (Kim et al., 2026), while AgentCARD maps deployment-aware cost–accuracy frontiers over
multi-role assignments (Jiang et al., 2026). These systems characterize configuration landscapes
after evaluation.

Efficient LLM evaluation. BanditEval exploits low-rank response-matrix structure (Zhou et al.,
2025), SySRs uses paired comparisons to identify the best model (Lyu et al., 2026), and PromptEval
shares information across prompts and examples to estimate multi-prompt performance (Polo et al.,
2024). Gittins indices have also been adapted to account for evaluation costs in LLM model selec-
tion (Xie et al., 2026). These methods focus on scalar performance estimation or single-objective
model selection; CC-G ITTINS extends cost-aware Gittins search to multi-objective Pareto recom-
mendation for complete agent workflows.

Multi-objective LLM configuration optimization. SCOPE and VineLM consider multiple ob-
jectives through constrained optimization: SCOPE minimizes deployment cost subject to a qual-
ity threshold (Huang et al., 2026), while VineLM maximizes accuracy under cost or latency bud-
gets (Pagonas et al., 2026). Concurrent work by Xue et al. (2026) uses a frequentist approach:
empirical gap elimination for Pareto identification and hypervolume-per-cost UCB for online LLM
configuration selection. Its experiments vary models, prompts, and decoding settings for accuracy–
latency trade-offs under token-based evaluation budgets. We take a Bayesian approach to joint
model assignment across interacting agent roles, using Gittins indices to guide cost-aware search
while maintaining anytime quality–deployment-cost recommendations. Our method exploits the
coupling between search and deployment costs.

Pareto-set search. General-purpose multi-objective bandits study Pareto-set identification under
fixed-confidence or fixed-budget objectives (Kone et al., 2023; 2024). Hypervolume-based Bayesian

3.1   H OW EVALUATION PRICES GUIDE DEPLOYMENT TRADE - OFFS

Deployment cost affects both the quality–cost trade-off and the cost of evaluating an assignment. For
comparable benchmark and deployment queries, evaluating a batch of B queries for configuration
i costs approximately κi ≈ BDi . We use a positive estimate κ        bi , fixed after warm-up, to select
evaluations, while actual API charges contribute to the cumulative search cost St . These costs may

3.3   C ONTROLLER S CHEDULING AND A SYNCHRONOUS COST RELAXATION

The cost (C) and quality (Q) controllers alternate turns in the order C, Q. Each controller a ∈
{C, Q} uses a multiplier ηa , initially one, to scale the estimated evaluation cost κbi in its Gittins
index (Eq. 9). On its turn, the controller compares the largest index among unfinished configurations
with its best completed terminal value Ia (set Ia = −∞ if none is complete). If an unfinished
configuration is preferred, the controller evaluates one batch and updates the shared posteriors.
Otherwise, the controller would stop at its current evaluation price. Instead, we halve only its mul-
tiplier ηa when possible, reducing the penalty for future evaluations. The other controller’s multi-
plier and actual API charges remain unchanged. The search terminates when both controllers reach
stopping conditions that cannot be relaxed without an intervening evaluation, an external budget
is reached, or all configurations are complete. The round-robin schedule balances controller turns
rather than evaluation counts or API cost. Appendix B provides the numerical stopping rules.

3.4   W HY COST COUPLING EXPOSES QUALITY– COST TRADE - OFFS

At a fixed uncertainty schedule and remaining horizon, raising the information price lowers the
scalar index (Appendix A). Coupling makes that price depend on a deployment objective. To isolate
its role, consider a deterministic completion model: outcomes are known, but terminal utility still
requires paying for the remaining evaluation batches.
Proposition 1 (Deterministic roles of the two exact axes). Suppose uncertainty has vanished, each
unfinished configuration requires the same h remaining full batches of size B to collect terminal
utility, and each batch costs exactly κi = BDi . Among unfinished configurations,

Thus the quality axis ranks a quality–cost scalarization, while the cost axis ranks configurations by
increasing deployment cost.

Coupled prices favor a more expensive assignment when ∆Q > λQ ∆D; lowering ηQ relaxes this
requirement at fixed h. This can expose supported trade-offs, meaning frontier points maximizing
Qi − λDi for some λ ≥ 0, while the cost axis targets the inexpensive endpoint. Equal evaluation
prices would preserve the quality ranking. Bayesian uncertainty, unequal remaining horizons, and
estimated prices break this exact scalarization interpretation. Discrete price relaxation need not visit
every supported point, while unsupported points lie outside this linear argument. Thus the policy
has no complete-frontier recovery guarantee.

4     E XPERIMENTS

4.1   E XPERIMENTAL SETUP

Benchmarks. Our quality–cost evaluation uses eight exhaustive or replay-complete matrices
across five workflow families. We evaluate 102 = 100 assignments on all 200 questions of
each of HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018) (planner and search-enabled solver) and MathQA (Amini
et al., 2019) (answerer and critic). Six further instances use SCOPE’s released query-level evalu-
ations (Huang et al., 2026), covering three-to-five-role text-to-SQL, data-transformation, and data-
integration workflows. These configurations form subsets of the nominal assignment spaces, each
evaluated on every benchmark question. All methods replay the same complete score–cost matrices
(Appendix D.1).

Evaluation protocol and metrics. The offline replay reveals only purchased observations. CC-
G ITTINS uses four warm questions per configuration, batches of up to four unseen questions, and
two exact-axis controllers with independent price relaxation. Both quality and deployment-cost prior
variances are estimated from warm-start observations. Comparisons use 20 matched question-order
seeds (42–61) and charge observed API costs, including warm-up. Metric scales are fixed across
methods and seeds. We report HV regret, GD, and IGD (Section 2), dividing HV regret by HV(P ⋆ )
for the quality–cost plots; lower is better.

Baselines. We compare CC-G ITTINS with EGE-SH for fixed-budget Pareto-set identifica-
tion (Kone et al., 2024), the adaptive Pareto-exploration sampling rule of APE-k (Kone et al., 2023),
and qNEHVI, a Bayesian optimization method that selects evaluations by noisy expected hypervol-
ume improvement (Daulton et al., 2021). We also compare CC-G ITTINS with two random search
baselines: random questions, which evaluates all assignments on the same randomly drawn bench-
mark questions, and random configurations, which fully evaluates randomly drawn assignments.
Appendix D.4 gives their replay protocols.

5           D ISCUSSION AND C ONCLUSIONS

CC-G ITTINS exploits the coupling between deployment and evaluation costs for anytime multi-
objective Pareto recommendation. Across eight quality–cost benchmarks, it recovers substantial
portions of the exhaustive Pareto frontier at low evaluation cost. Experiments including latency also
demonstrate applicability to three-objective Pareto recommendation.
We consider static role-level assignments under fixed orchestration. Complete frontier recovery is
not guaranteed, and controller design remains empirical. Future work could adapt model choices,
retries, and execution paths to intermediate outcomes.

D.4     BASELINE IMPLEMENTATIONS

All five baselines use the same frozen response matrices but follow their own sampling protocols;
checkpoints are aligned by realized cumulative API search cost. EGE-SH allocates observations in
rounds and uses empirical Pareto gaps to halve the active assignments (Kone et al., 2024). We use the
confidence-bound sampling rule of APE-k to direct observations toward uncertain Pareto compar-
isons; its fixed-confidence stopping condition is disabled, and sampling continues until the replay
observation cap is reached or available observations are exhausted (Kone et al., 2023). qNEHVI
uses a Bayesian surrogate over categorical assignment features and chooses evaluations by noisy
expected hypervolume improvement (Daulton et al., 2021).
The two random search baselines differ in the sampled unit: random questions evaluates all as-
signments on the same randomly drawn questions, while random configurations fully evaluates ran-
domly drawn assignments. Both recommend the empirical Pareto set from their observed outcomes.

Protocol. We center four one-factor families and a 2 × 2 prior-variance study (Fig. 18) on G0,
the CC-G ITTINS protocol with real per-configuration acquisition costs, independent direction-local
η decay, and round-robin axis visits. The cost family compares G0 with quality-only real cost
(G1), quality-only unit cost (G2), and exact axes with unit cost (G3). The direction family includes
quality-only G1, deployment-only G4, G0, axes plus the midpoint (G5), and five evenly spaced
directions (G6), all with real costs. The relaxation family compares G0’s independent direction-local
relaxation with the fixed-η no-stop control (G7). The scheduler family compares G0 with quality-
then-deployment (G8) and deployment-then-quality (G9), which drain one axis before visiting the
other.
Each configuration uses the same eight benchmark matrices, 20 seeds, model ordering, exhaustive
truth vectors, recommendation rule, batch size, and cost checkpoints spanning 0–30% of exhaustive
API evaluation cost. The frontier-metric grid contains thirteen configurations and 2,080 validated
benchmark–seed runs. At each fixed 0.5-percentage-point target, we score each contributing seed’s
last recommendation not exceeding that cost. Curves show seed means and bands show ±2 standard
errors. HV regret, GD, and IGD are all lower-is-better and use the same frozen per-benchmark
metric space as the primary comparison.

Cost mechanism. Figure 13 separates acquisition pricing from controller choice. G0 versus G3
and G1 versus G2 compare real with unit prices; the comparisons retain deployment-cost observa-
tions, the Pareto recommendation rule, and realized-dollar accounting. G0 versus G1 and G3 versus
G2 test adding the cost controller at fixed pricing. Real prices align acquisition with monetary
spend; the cost controller targets inexpensive assignments. These controls support combining both
mechanisms, with benchmark-dependent gains. They assess pricing and controller choices within
the coupled setting, rather than isolating the effect of the relation κi = BDi itself.

Directions. Figure 14 shows that extra directions redistribute evaluation effort rather than uni-
formly improving the recommendation. Intermediate controllers can concentrate evidence on a
compact set of high-value trade-offs, improving dominated-volume recovery and recommendation
proximity while weakening uniform frontier coverage. The one-axis controls show the benefit of
retaining both endpoint-oriented controllers, while adding directions does not mechanically enlarge
the final set because all controllers share observations and a common conservative recommenda-
tion rule forms the returned set. The frontier-metric curves compare variants at matched fractions
of exhaustive API evaluation cost, whereas the runtime experiment fixes the number of evaluated
matrix entries at ⌈0.30KN ⌉, where K is the number of configurations and N is the number of ques-
tions in the benchmark matrix. The latter measures policy computation at equal evaluation counts
rather than equal search cost. Including the single-direction controls, the runtime experiment covers
five settings (Fig. 15). G0 has lower mean policy runtime than both denser direction sets on all
eight benchmarks. Averaging per-benchmark runtime ratios, G5 and G6 require 1.39× and 1.74×
G0’s policy time, respectively. Together with the frontier-quality controls, these results support the
two-axis configuration as a trade-off between frontier recovery and policy computation.

Relaxation. Figure 16 compares G0’s independent direction-local relaxation with the fixed-η no-
stop control G7. In G0, a local stopping signal triggers a reduction of the visited controller’s
information-price multiplier when possible, allowing configurations whose value of information
was marginal at an earlier price scale to become eligible for further evaluation. G7 instead retains
the initial price scale and continues without a Gittins stop. G0 achieves lower aggregate mean HV
regret, GD, and IGD at the 30% checkpoint. These results support the relaxation mechanism for
improving volume recovery and frontier coverage while maintaining competitive recommendation
proximity; the gains vary across benchmarks.

Scheduler. Figure 17 shows that controller order affects recommendation quality at matched cu-
mulative search cost: a sequential policy allocates early evaluations to its first controller and post-
pones evidence about the opposite frontier extreme. Round-robin scheduling instead balances di-
rectional attention from the outset, while shared observations allow every purchased batch to inform
both controllers. This combination exposes both endpoints early enough for their evidence to shape
the interior recommendation, making interleaving more robust than draining either axis first.

Cost mechanism. Figure 19 separates acquisition pricing from controller choice. G1 versus G7,
G4 versus G8, and G0 versus G9 compare real with unit prices while retaining observed deployment
costs, the Pareto recommendation rule, and realized-dollar accounting. For the four-controller pol-
icy, using observed acquisition costs lowers HV regret and GD on both matrices at 30%. This result
supports the real-cost pricing used by G0 to allocate its evaluation budget.

Directions. Figure 20 compares how controller directions allocate evaluation effort. All con-
trollers observe the same three outcomes, which a common recommendation rule uses to form
the returned set. G4 interleaves the three pure axes; G0 adds the Q–L midpoint with radial util-
ity min{Qi , ℓi }. G5 combines the Q–L endpoints, quarter points, and midpoint with the D axis; G6
combines the three axes with the Q–L, L–D, and Q–D midpoints. Round-robin scheduling balances
decision opportunities, not purchased batches or dollars. At 30%, G0 has lower HV regret than
each single-direction control on both matrices. Its four directions represent all three objective axes
and the quality–latency interaction with fewer controllers than the six-direction variants, providing
a compact main configuration.
Figure 21 compares policy computation at an equal number of evaluated entries. G0 takes 13.3
seconds on HotpotQA and 12.5 seconds on MathQA, within the 10.0–15.0-second range of the
tested direction sets. Together with its low HV regret at 30% of evaluation cost (Figure 20), this
runtime supports using four directions as the main three-objective configuration.

Relaxation. Figure 22 isolates the continuation rule under the same four controllers and round-
robin schedule. G0 independently relaxes each direction’s evaluation-cost penalty after local stop-
ping signals, whereas G10 holds η = 1 fixed. This direction-local adaptation retains the stopping
behavior used by the main anytime policy; the curves show how the alternative continuation rules
affect the three frontier metrics on each benchmark.
