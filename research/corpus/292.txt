                                                                                       A BSTRACT




arXiv:2610.27822v1 [cs.AI] 20 Oct 2026
                                                      LLM routing aims to select a capable model at low cost, but specialized routers
                                                      can require updates as models and tasks change. We propose COMPASS, an
                                                      agentic router in which an LLM follows a routing recipe, gathers performance
                                                      and cost evidence through tools, and optionally inspects candidate answers before
                                                      selecting a model. Our evaluations on LLMRouterBench and DeepSWE show
                                                      that agentic routing can improve accuracy and lower model-call cost relative to
                                                      specialized and trained routing baselines, without routing-specific training. On
                                                      LLMRouterBench, COMPASS reaches 69.85% accuracy at $139.57, compared
                                                      with RLCascadeRouter’s 69.14% at $180.22. On DeepSWE under fixed five-fold
                                                      cross-validation, it reaches 78.1% at $215, compared with 74.1% at $500 for the
                                                      best single model. COMPASS also supports routing across benchmarks and adapts
                                                      to an expanded model pool by reading new evaluation records, without changing
                                                      the router’s weights or prompt.


                                         1       I NTRODUCTION
                                         The ecosystem of large language models has fragmented into distinct capability–price tiers. Frontier
                                         models dominate benchmarks but are expensive and often slow; small open-weight models are cheap
                                         and fast, and for many task distributions they are entirely sufficient. Consider a user requesting a
                                         simple Python script: this task does not require a frontier model, and a cost-effective model would
                                         complete it at a fraction of the price. The difficulty is that this knowledge is available only in
                                         hindsight: one would need to query both the cheap and the frontier model to know whether the
                                         cheap model actually sufficed. The routing problem is precisely the problem of estimating this
                                         counterfactual before committing to a model.
                                         Routing must navigate a three-way trade-off:

                                                    • Cost. Cheaper models reduce per-token spend, but only if they succeed; failed attempts
                                                      followed by escalation can cost more than routing correctly the first time.
                                                    • Latency. A cheap model with low throughput that emits many tokens can take far longer
                                                      end-to-end than a stronger model that solves the task in substantially fewer tokens. Cost-
                                                      optimal and latency-optimal choices frequently diverge.
                                                    • Accuracy. Some users simply want the strongest available response regardless of price.

                                         Most prior work optimizes a single axis, usually cost reduction at near-constant quality, because cost
                                         is the easiest axis to measure and experiment on (Chen et al., 2024a; Ong et al., 2025). Optimizing
                                         for accuracy instead pushes evaluation toward frontier models, where per-experiment API costs are
                                         substantial and public fine-grained evaluation data is scarce.

                                         Routing recipes. Users can specify what they value without estimating how difficult a query will
                                         be. A performance-focused recipe asks the router to prioritize reliable completion; a cost-optimized

recipe asks it to seek cheaper configurations when the evidence supports comparable quality. Qual-
itative levels such as low, medium, or high can express the strength of these preferences. The recipe
provides guidelines for gathering evidence and making a decision, while the router assesses the
query using that evidence. Numerical trade-off parameters can still be used inside tools and experi-
mental sweeps; users need not tune them for each request.

The staleness problem. When model-specific performance estimates are encoded in a trained
router, changes in the model pool or task distribution can require new supervision or updates. Meth-
ods that support unseen models reduce this burden, but still need useful evidence about their ca-
pabilities. We instead keep evaluation outcomes in an external corpus that the router can query at
inference time. New models can be added by collecting and inserting their evaluation records, with-
out updating the routing agent’s weights. This shifts the maintenance burden to evidence collection,
whose cost and coverage remain important limitations.

Our proposal. We separate routing knowledge from routing skill. Knowledge about which model
scored what, at what cost, and on which task lives in an external evaluation corpus. The skill to
query that corpus and decide under a routing recipe comes from the tool-use capabilities of an
existing LLM. COMPASS requires no routing-specific weight training: the agent follows the recipe,
adaptively explores evidence, and submits a result when it judges the evidence sufficient. This yields
three design requirements:

      1. Recalibration through evidence. New models and benchmarks are incorporated by in-
         serting evaluation records into the corpus; the router’s weights are untouched.
      2. Recipe-oriented routing. The router follows input guidelines, including performance-
         focused and cost-optimized recipes with qualitative preference levels.
      3. Low routing overhead. Evidence acquisition and agent inference must be economical
         enough to preserve any savings in model execution.

Contributions.

      1. We formulate routing as evidence gathering through tools, separating updatable model
         records from an existing LLM’s decision-making capabilities (Section 3).
      2. We implement COMPASS, which follows routing recipes to gather evidence, inspect can-
         didate answers, and select a model or stop unresolved (Section 4).
      3. We evaluate COMPASS against routing baselines on LLMRouterBench and under five-
         fold cross-validation on DeepSWE. COMPASS reaches 78.1% accuracy on DeepSWE and
         76.7% using only TerminalBench evidence. Expanding the model pool improves accuracy
         and lowers model-call spend without retraining; ablations measure the effects of router
         choice and model names (Section 5).


2   R ELATED W ORK

Trainable model routing. One-step routers learn to select a model or execution configuration
from preferences and difficulty estimates (Ong et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2024), predicted perfor-
mance (Xue et al., 2026; Fernandez et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2024b; Zhuang et al., 2024), query–model
representations (Chen et al., 2024b; Feng et al., 2024; Jitkrittum et al., 2026), online feedback (Panda
et al., 2025), or latent expert interactions (Fein-Ashley et al., 2025). They commit before inspecting
a completed answer and therefore cannot revise that query’s route using post-generation evidence.
Sequential systems can instead purchase more precise estimates (Fisch et al., 2026), inspect an an-
swer before stopping or escalating (Chen et al., 2024a; Shah & Shridhar, 2025; Chuang et al., 2024;
Dekoninck et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2024; Aggarwal et al., 2024), or learn multi-call orchestra-
tion (Nielsen et al., 2026; Shao et al., 2025). Across both families, trained predictors, scorers, and
policies may need refreshed outcomes or updates as model pools and task distributions change.
Some methods explicitly support new models, but the reliability of transfer across pools remains an
empirical question.

Training-free model routing. Training-free routers reuse stored query–model outcomes without
fitting a dedicated neural policy. The Avengers, Avengers-Pro, and UniRoute’s k-means variant
summarize model performance in prompt clusters (Zhang et al., 2025b;a; Jitkrittum et al., 2026).
Nearest-neighbor variants in RouterBench and CARROT, together with benchmark-based and on-
line kNN routers, transfer observed success or inference-cost estimates from similar evaluated
queries (Hu et al., 2024; Shnitzer et al., 2023; Somerstep et al., 2025; Li, 2025; Wu & Silwal,
2025). Eagle blends global and local Elo rankings (Zhao et al., 2024), while ProxRouter reweights
nearby evidence to improve outlier robustness (Patel et al., 2025). The quality of these estimates
depends on the coverage and relevance of stored examples: when neighbors are sparse, noisy, or
close in embedding space yet different in difficulty or required capability, local model rankings can
be unstable or misleading. Global rankings and proximity weights can reduce this risk, but a weakly
supported query may still require other evidence.
These approaches expose two sources of fragility: trainable predictors and policies may require
new supervision or retraining as model pools change, and their transfer to unseen pools remains
uncertain; neighbor-based training-free estimates can become unreliable when relevant examples
are sparse, weakly related, or noisy. COMPASS separates model-specific knowledge from routing
skill: outcomes and inference costs reside in an updatable evaluation corpus, while an existing
LLM agent uses anonymized model identities to decide which evidence to acquire. Adding a model
requires calibration outcomes rather than changes to the agent’s weights. Neighbor retrieval is only
one optional evidence tool; the agent can also consult model profiles and quality–cost estimates,
inspect a generated answer, request further evidence, or stop unresolved. More informative tools
can enrich this search as they become available, provided their inference cost and effect on final-
answer reliability are assessed.

4      COMPASS
COMPASS gives an existing LLM a routing prompt (a recipe), evaluation corpus E, candidate list,
and accuracy–cost preference α ∈ [0, 1]. For each query, it gathers evidence and submits a configu-
ration (m, e) (a model alias and reasoning-effort level) with free-text evidence and a self-reported,
uncalibrated confidence label. Different effort levels are separate choices with measured pass rates
and costs. No routing-specific weights are trained.

Tools have individual call limits; identical calls are deduplicated, and recipes allow 4–16 calls to-
tal. Reading corpus evidence adds no model-call cost. Each preview is charged even if rejected.
Precomputed tables reduced calls from six to two or three per task while maintaining or improving
accuracy: the agent compares evidence, decides whether to preview, and stops instead of rebuilding
statistics.

4.2    ROUTING RECIPES

Recipes specify the objective, tool order, and selection rule. We evaluate four families:

        • Single pick: read reliability or neighbourhood evidence, choose from its accuracy–cost
          frontier using α, and submit; one or two calls, no previews.
        • Cascade: start with the kNN recommendation, inspect its answer, and accept or try the
          next candidate (Section 3).
        • Conditional preview: allow all evidence sources, but enable previews only under preset
          conditions, such as tied leaders, conflicting evidence, or benchmark diagnostics.
        • Choose a policy: compare predefined policies with backtest policy, then apply one.
          The agent cannot invent policies.

Shared prompt segments keep selection rules identical across arms. Paraphrasing the single-pick
rule changed DeepSWE pass@1 by 5.5 points; replacing its informal trade-off with an executable
objective changed it by 9.5 points.

4.3    M ODEL IDENTITIES AND UPDATES

Aliases are generated per run and resolved outside the agent. They hide names and reputation, al-
though scores, prices, or output style may reveal identity (Section 5.7.1). Adding candidates requires
only their records and a candidate-list update, with the router’s weights unchanged (Section 5.6).

4.4     E XECUTION AND EVALUATION

Sessions end on submission or budget exhaustion; exhausted sessions are unresolved and retried
only under the transient-failure policy. Task cost includes every previewed configuration and the
submitted configuration at their measured per-attempt costs; router inference is reported separately.
Corpus-only recipes score the chosen configuration’s mean pass rate; cascades score the inspected,
accepted attempt. We report these scoring conventions separately (Section 5).
Evidence and evaluation tasks are disjoint. Visible records supply COMPASS’s evidence and base-
line training data, never router weight updates. Tables are rebuilt per fold; removing held-out tasks
must leave them byte-identical. We study one routing decision per task, not model switching within
a conversation.


5     E XPERIMENTS

5.1     E VALUATION DATA

Routing cannot be evaluated from leaderboard aggregates. A router makes a decision per task,
so evaluating it requires, for every task and candidate model, whether the model solved the task
and how much the attempt cost. This per-task record is the scarce resource and determines which
benchmarks we can use.
Our main routing evaluations use three sources. LLMRouterBench (Li et al., 2026) provides pre-
collected per-prompt, per-model results for thirteen models across ten benchmarks: AIME (Math-
ematical Association of America, n.d.), ArenaHard (Li et al., 2024), GPQA (Rein et al., 2023),
Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE) (Phan et al., 2025), LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2024), LiveMath-
Bench (Liu et al., 2024a), MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024), SimpleQA (Wei et al., 2024), SWE-
bench (Jimenez et al., 2023), and τ 2 -Bench (TAU2) (Barres et al., 2025). We use its released record
dump with the performance–cost configuration’s filters. It is also the corpus used for our head-to-
head baseline comparison. Its main limitation is that it contains one record per prompt and model:
there are no repeated trials, so pass@k and per-task variance cannot be measured.
DeepSWE (Huang et al., 2026) and TerminalBench (Merrill et al., 2026) publish traces. Their
repeated trials per cell (four on DeepSWE) make the pass@k and ceiling analysis in §5.5 possible,
since we can split trials into disjoint evidence and evaluation sets. The traces also let the router
inspect what a model did rather than only whether it passed. Our DeepSWE catalog contains 31 617
trials over 113 tasks and 28 model/effort configurations. The paired TerminalBench cohort contains
66 tasks on an exact 18-configuration roster.
For the ceiling analysis in Section 5.5, we also use repeated-trial records from FrontierSWE (Kon-
dra et al., 2026).

5.2.2                       R ESULTS
Figure 2 shows how COMPASS trades model-call cost for accuracy. Its most accurate setting reaches
69.85% at $139.57, compared with the plotted RLCascadeRouter reference at 69.14% and $180.22.
COMPASS also reaches 69.36% at $128.30, exceeding GPT-5’s 65.12% at a similar cost of $124.82.
At a lower cost, COMPASS reaches 68.37% at $110.69. The best recorded Avengers-Pro set-
ting reaches 66.79% at $91.50, while FrugalGPT reaches 66.89% at $226.54 and GraphRouter
reaches 65.31% at $157.79. COMPASS therefore provides the highest accuracy in the figure, while
Avengers-Pro remains competitive at lower budgets. These costs exclude router inference.

5.3                        D EEP SWE: FIVE - FOLD CROSS - VALIDATION

DeepSWE has only 113 tasks, so a 70/30 split leaves a small test set and produces highly variable
results. We therefore use five-fold cross-validation, holding each evaluation task out of the routing
evidence and keeping COMPASS’s policy fixed across folds. Accuracy averages the recorded trial
outcomes: one success in four trials contributes 25%.
Figure 3a shows COMPASS at 78.1%, compared with 74.1% for the best single model
(gpt-6-astra/xhigh), 69.9% for Avengers-Pro, and 72.8% for FrugalGPT. COMPASS uses
$215 in model-call spend, compared with $500 for the best single model. The accuracy estimates
favor COMPASS, although the confidence intervals remain wide on this small task set.

5.5              H OW MUCH CAN ROUTING IMPROVE ACCURACY ?

Routing helps only when different models are reliably better on different tasks. To estimate this
opportunity, we use repeated trials on DeepSWE and FrontierSWE: one group of trials selects a
model, and a disjoint group evaluates it. Across 60 random splits, we compare selection using
evidence from the target task itself, the most behaviorally similar other task, and a random task
against always using the best single model.
On DeepSWE, evidence from the task itself yields a 9.0-point gain, while evidence from the most
similar other task yields 4.0 points (Figure 4 in Appendix A). This suggests useful routing opportu-
nities, but only part of that opportunity is accessible through related tasks. On FrontierSWE, neither
approach beats the best single model, leaving little measured benefit from per-task selection.
The point is to distinguish apparent oracle gains from gains a router can actually recover. Choosing
and scoring a model on the same outcomes exaggerates the opportunity by selecting lucky trials.
LLMRouterBench has only one record per task and model, so it cannot support this disjoint-trial
check; its oracle gap alone is not evidence of achievable routing gains.

5.6              G ENERALIZATION TO A NEW MODEL POOL

We expand the DeepSWE catalog from 18 to 28 models by adding their evaluation records,
then rerun COMPASS on the same 113 tasks under fixed five-fold cross-validation. With
gpt-5.6-luna/medium as the router, accuracy rises from 63.9% to 78.1%, while model-call
spend falls from $474 to $215. The routing model, prompt, and policy remain fixed: COMPASS
adopts the new models directly from their records, without retraining.

5.7              A BLATIONS

5.7.1                D OES ANONYMITY MATTER ?
We compare opaque aliases with real model names while keeping the router, prompt, candidate pool,
and tasks fixed. On LLMRouterBench, revealing names changes macro accuracy by −0.49 points


                                                                          8
arXiv preprint




for gpt-5.6-luna and +0.47 points for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. These single-run comparisons
show small, opposite changes, with no consistent accuracy benefit from revealing model identities.
Names nevertheless affect the choices: Luna changes 19% of its selections, compared with 6% for
DeepSeek. Both shift slightly toward GPT-5, whose selection share rises from 35.7% to 37.8%
for Luna and from 33.6% to 34.1% for DeepSeek. This suggests that model-specific priors can
influence routing even when aggregate accuracy barely changes. Anonymization removes explicit
model names as a cue for memorized reputation or flagship preference, encouraging decisions based
on the supplied evidence.

5.7.2    H OW MUCH DOES THE ROUTING MODEL MATTER ?

We swap the routing LLM while keeping the agent, prompt, objective, candidate pool, and tasks
fixed. On the LLMRouterBench ablation, gpt-5.6-luna, gpt-5.6-sol, and DeepSeek-V4.1-
Flash differ by only 1.15 macro accuracy points. DeepSeek selects models with 22% less model-call
spend than Luna, at a cost of 0.86 accuracy points. Changing the router therefore affects spending
more than accuracy in this setting; these costs exclude the router’s own inference bill.
Because these LLMs have not been trained for our routing objective, they can interpret the same
cost–accuracy preference differently. DeepSeek tends to favor cheaper configurations, which can
sacrifice accuracy when the model pool offers a wider range of choices. On DeepSWE with fixed
five-fold CV, it reaches 68.4% at $105, compared with Luna’s 78.1% at $215. Router choice thus
matters more in this setting: lower model-call spend can come with a substantial accuracy loss.


6   L IMITATIONS

        • Existing LLMs as routing agents. COMPASS uses an existing LLM; we have not trained
          a specialized model for routing. The results motivate investigating such training as a next
          step, particularly to improve evidence use and reduce inference overhead, but do not estab-
          lish that it will improve performance.
        • Evaluation cost and coverage. Collecting per-task outcomes and trajectories across mod-
          els is expensive. Routing quality depends on the coverage, reliability, and relevance of
          those records. Adding a model requires new evidence even though it does not require re-
          training the agent, and sparse or mismatched data can lead to poor choices.
        • Distribution-dependent routing. Different task distributions have different routing pro-
          files, and the same router does not perform best across all datasets. Strong results on one
          cohort do not establish reliable transfer to another.
        • Routing overhead and uncertainty. Agent inference can consume the savings from
          cheaper model choices. DeepSWE estimates have wide confidence intervals, and most
          experiments use one routing realization per task. The reported task-bootstrap intervals
          therefore do not capture all routing stochasticity.


7   C ONCLUSION

COMPASS uses an existing LLM to follow routing recipes and adaptively gather evidence from an
updatable evaluation corpus. This separation lets new models be incorporated without retraining
the routing agent. Experiments on LLMRouterBench and DeepSWE demonstrate useful accuracy–
cost tradeoffs, while the routing-cost analysis and cross-benchmark transfer results show that gains
depend on both the evidence and the task distribution.
A next step is cache-aware multi-turn routing, in which the router revisits its decision as a session
reveals more about the task. Switching models can incur fresh context-processing costs and lose
caching benefits, so a useful policy must weigh these costs against the expected benefit of continu-
ing, switching, or delegating a subtask. Evaluating such policies requires session trajectories with
recorded switching decisions, outcomes, and costs. Constructing and evaluating this data is future
work; the present experiments address individual tasks.
