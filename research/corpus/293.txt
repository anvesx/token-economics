1   Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are now used at scale in software development, customer service,
and other knowledge-intensive work. As these applications expand, finite budgets for model use
have become a central operating constraint. Developer tools and model platforms typically
define these budgets over rolling or monthly usage periods and meter them in dollars or tokens
[Git26, Ant26]. Providers differ sharply in capability and price, so the same budget can support
materially different amounts of service depending on which models are used. A call’s final charge
is also uncertain at admission, since it depends in part on the length of the response. Together,
finite budgets, price dispersion, and uncertain call costs create an operational opportunity for
routing. By matching each task to a capable model whose cost fits the remaining budget, users
complete more work before the budget is exhausted.
    Much of the literature on LLM routing treats model choice as a quality–cost trade-off for the
current request. Cascades decide whether to escalate an inexpensive response, while supervised
routers learn which model is likely to deliver suﬀicient quality at an acceptable expected cost
[CZZ24, AMA+ 24, DMW+ 24, OAW+ 25, SPdO+ 26]. This expected-value perspective is natural
when requests can be considered independently or spending is controlled on average. However,
a finite pathwise budget in most LLM services is enforced based on cumulative realized spending
along the actual sequence of calls, rather than on expected spending. This stopping rule changes
the consequences of call-cost uncertainty. A call’s realized charge becomes known only after
completion, and unusually long responses are diﬀicult to anticipate. One call can therefore
exhaust the remaining budget and end service for every later task. Its consequence includes
both the current charge and the loss of future service. Optimizing expected quality against
expected cost request by request does not control this stopping risk.
    Consider a developer with a $100 monthly budget for model calls on OpenRouter. Coding
tasks arrive one at a time, from a short unit-test fix to a diﬀicult multi-file change. For each
task, the developer chooses among models including GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 5, Kimi K3,
GLM 5.2, and others. The models differ in price and in the kinds of tasks they solve well
[Ope26c, Ope26d]. Only after a call finishes does the developer observe its total token count


                                               2
and exact provider charge. If the response fails, the developer must decide whether another call,
now carrying the failed exchange in a longer prompt, is worth its cost or whether to abandon
the task and preserve budget for later work. Each selected call reveals its model’s outcome and
response length, whereas the outcomes of unselected models remain unknown. The challenge is
to learn which model fits each task while preserving enough budget to complete as many later
tasks as possible.
    The same operating structure arises when firms assign API budgets to employees, when
an LLM customer-service system handles cases of different diﬀiculty, and when a metered AI
service allocates model access across client requests. We focus on settings with three features.
Tasks are processed online under a pathwise budget. Call cost is unknown at admission and
can vary substantially in its upper tail. Success and cost profiles must be learned from selected
calls. These features lead to our central operations question: how should an operator route and
retry a sequential task stream to maximize the number of tasks completed before a finite LLM
budget stops service?
    To answer this question, we formulate LLM routing as a K-bit Pathwise Contextual Bandits
with Knapsacks problem and develop ADP-HT (Adaptive Dual-Pace Hybrid-Threshold), an
online policy that weighs each call’s chance of solving the current task against the future service
its uncertain cost puts at risk. Our contributions are fourfold.
    First, we formalize LLM routing under a budget with a halt-after-crossing rule, treating
each model as an arm. A successful response removes a task from the stream, a failed task may
be retried or abandoned, and the first call whose charge crosses the budget ends service. The
objective is to maximize the number of tasks solved before this stopping time. LLM’s response
lengths are heavy-tailed and diﬀicult to predict [WQX+ 26], and a single long response may
exhaust the remaining budget and terminate service for all future tasks. For such a halt-after-
crossing budget, the cost needs to be forecast in a risk-aware manner rather than an expected
point estimate sense. We therefore introduce a K-bit cost-shape channel, which discretizes
response length into 2K intervals and estimates the exceedance probability at each interval
boundary. This changes the cost prediction problem from estimating the exact response length
to estimating whether the response length would be longer or shorter, which is more robust
and reliable. A larger K improves the resolution of prediction but may increase the estimation
diﬀiculty, which introduces a trade-off between resolution and precision. This structure extends
the random-cost, halt-after-crossing formulation of [CES20] from repeated arm pulls to an online
task stream with per-task value, router-controlled retries, and K-bit cost prediction.
    Second, we give an implementable online policy for choosing models and retries. A hybrid
reward learner combines a shared low-dimensional component with model-specific adjustments
to form an optimistic solve probability. Threshold-survival heads use the quantized targets to
estimate a pre-call cost bound at a chosen tail tolerance. The selector subtracts that shadow-
priced cost bound from the optimistic solve probability, so an expensive model remains eligible
when its chance of solving the task compensates for the budget it would consume. A dual-pace
controller adjusts the shadow price from realized spending and smooths the pacing response
through recent average costs. After a failure, the retry gate raises its margin with the attempt
index and spending beyond the revised task allowance.
    Third, we characterize the K-bit trade-off between resolution and precision and show that
its discretization and estimation terms are jointly order-tight under the stated confidence cal-
ibration. The implemented grid divides a core range containing the more common response
lengths into quantile-based intervals and reserves one tail interval for the remaining long re-
sponses. Our main theorem separates the excess cost allowance arising from grid coarseness,
warm-data placement of the grid edges, and simultaneous estimation of the threshold-crossing
probabilities. More bits reduce the discretization term but increase the estimation allowance
at a fixed sample size. The result applies when the target response-length threshold, including
its estimation allowance, remains in the core. The same argument extends to other fixed grids


                                                3
through their local edge spacing. We also ask how the value of tail protection changes with the
remaining workload even when cost distributions are known, by solving a two-model special-
ization with one call per task. At a fixed budget, the optimal first choice can move from the
risky model to the predictable model and then back as the number of remaining tasks increases.
Predictable calls protect future service, but their higher ordinary cost can displace useful calls
when many tasks remain.
    Fourth, three numerical experiments evaluate the policy, from benchmark task streams to a
deployed commercial router. At the tightest tested budgets, ADP-HT solves an average of 216.2
MATH500 problems and 85.0 GPQA questions, more than twice the best implemented online
comparator in each dataset. On RouterBench, the implemented ADP-HT policy leads in the
descriptive ranking at all eight tight budgets and ranks first in ten of fourteen budgets overall. In
the commercial experiment, ADP-HT’s test phase solves 682 problems while incurring $0.8084,
whereas default AutoRouter’s complete run solves 623 for $22.86.
    The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the related literature to
situate our formulation among bandits with resource constraints, tail-aware learning, and cost-
aware LLM routing. Section 3 defines the formal model including the task stream, exact budget
ledger, and stopping rule. Section 4 develops ADP-HT, which prices every call and retry
against the budget it puts at risk and characterizes the K-bit trade-off in pre-call cost precision.
Section 5 evaluates the policy in three experiments, from live routing with retries to cross-
task allocation on a public benchmark and a comparison with a deployed commercial router.
Section 6 concludes.

2     Literature Review
Our work draws from and contributes to three streams of literature, which we connect by
examining how a depleting pathwise budget and coarsened tail-cost information jointly shape
model-choice and retry decisions.

2.1   Online Resource Allocation under Random Consumption
Online resource allocation provides the primary operations perspective for our problem. In
revenue management, bid prices express the opportunity cost of consuming capacity now rather
than reserving it for later demand [TvR98, Top09], and online-allocation policies extend this
logic by updating resource prices as demand and consumption unfold [BLM23]. The same logic
applies to an LLM budget. Every admitted call spends capacity that could otherwise serve a
later task, and our policy prices each call against that displaced service.
    Bandits with knapsacks bring learning into this opportunity-cost framework. Canonical
models combine selected-arm feedback with a finite resource [TTCRJ12, BKS18]. Contextual
and regression-oracle variants allow reward and consumption to depend on observed covariates
[AD16, SZSF24, ZKSV22]. Related learning problems arise in inventory control and reusable-
resource pricing [TDSY26, JSS22]. Random-cost formulations allow jointly distributed costs and
rewards that are observed after selection [CES20]. Our formulation retains the finite budget,
random consumption, and stopping upon depletion, and applies them to an online stream of
tasks.
    Within this stream, [CES20] provides a close starting point. Each pull generates a random
cost–reward pair, play ends when cumulative cost first exceeds the budget, and the last pull’s


                                                 4
reward is retained. Their model allows signed and potentially unbounded costs and rewards.
We focus on nonnegative token charges, stop when spending reaches or exceeds the budget,
and retain the final response’s reward. We add three operational features to this post-call
accounting. First, the allocation unit is an online stream of distinct tasks rather than repeated
arm pulls, so value is earned only when a task is completed and a verified task leaves the stream.
Second, continuation is itself a decision. After a failed call, the policy chooses whether another
call should compete with unserved tasks for the shared budget. Third, the cost predictor uses
quantized response-length targets to estimate the upper tail of the next call’s cost. This tail
matters for both model choice and continuation because a large realized charge can end service
for later tasks.

2.2   Tail-Sensitive Learning with Coarsened Cost Feedback
Risk-sensitive and safety-constrained bandits protect different aspects of sequential perfor-
mance. Conservative policies require cumulative performance to remain above a prescribed
fraction of a baseline, with guarantees in expectation or with high probability [WSLS16]. Re-
lated formulations address anytime cumulative constraints [LLSY21] and chance constraints on
resource use [CKMZ19, LNSZ21]. Gambler and survival bandits model termination at a ruin
threshold for accumulated reward or wealth [PVG+ 21, RHS24b]. Quantile forecasts describe
uncertainty in service outcomes [ATM23], while quantile-bandit and sequential-estimation meth-
ods estimate reward quantiles and identify arms by their quantile performance [ZO21, HR22].
Our focus is the conditional upper tail of the next call’s cost.
    A second stream shows that aggressively coarsened feedback can support effective shape
learning, which is operationally relevant when the exact cost is unknown at admission and
hard to predict. Communication-constrained bandits quantize rewards into a few bits while
preserving learning performance [HYF22], and one-bit quantile bandits use adaptive threshold
queries to identify the arm with the highest reward quantile [LS25]. We bring this coarsened-
feedback idea to cost-tail estimation. The exact provider charge updates the pathwise ledger,
while the cost learner receives only which of 2K fixed response-length intervals contains the
call. The resolution parameter K thereby becomes an information-design parameter, since a
finer mesh localizes the conditional tail more closely but enlarges the set of thresholds estimated
simultaneously, and our analysis characterizes this finite-sample trade-off for the algorithm we
propose.

2.3   Cost-Aware and Sequential LLM Routing
LLM routing methods differ in whether they enforce a budget through realized cumulative
spending across tasks and whether they jointly model uncertain call costs and retries. Cascades
such as FrugalGPT and AutoMix escalate from an inexpensive model when its response is judged
insuﬀicient [CZZ24, AMA+ 24]. HybridLLM, RouteLLM, and CARROT instead learn a request-
level choice along a quality–cost frontier [DMW+ 24, OAW+ 25, SPdO+ 26], and RouterBench
supplies an offline environment for comparing such routers [HBL+ 24]. Adaptive methods reach
further. PILOT allocates estimated costs over a known query set [PMD+ 25], [PDL+ 26] control
up to H calls within a query and screen models by confidence bounds on mean cost, Pareto-
Bandit paces an average per-request cost ceiling over an open-ended stream [TM26], ACQB
uses user retrials as implicit feedback for joint routing and scheduling [BSL26], and SeqRoute
and MTRouter learn offline from logged trajectories how a model choice bears on the terminal
outcome of a session [XZW26, ZLW+ 26]. [BTZ26] develop OSCAR and analyze which LLM
configurations to call and when to stop while searching for the next simulator-certified improve-
ment. Related stopping decisions arise in contextual search [BCW26] and LLM-based sequential
testing [LLL+ 26]. Our decision unit is instead a shared task-stream budget that binds pathwise
by halting, so a call’s consequence includes the future service its realized cost displaces, and the


                                                 5
exact cross-task ledger, the tail-cost bound for the next call, and router-controlled retries must
be modeled jointly. The router also learns online from chosen-arm feedback on verifier-checked
tasks rather than offline from logs.


3     Problem Statement
We consider a budget holder who calls LLMs directly under a single monetary or token amount
fixed for the period, such as the developer of Section 1. Tasks arrive online, one at a time, and
each is served with one of the available models. After a failed response, the decision maker may
retry with the failed exchange added to the prompt or leave the task for manual work. The
objective is to maximize the number of tasks completed before the budget is exhausted. Each
call both draws down the budget and generates data about the model that was called, so the
exploration–exploitation trade-off is central to the problem. A model’s output is observed only
after the call returns, and its length, the part of the charge that is unknown at admission, is
diﬀicult to predict accurately in advance, even for a fixed prompt on a fixed model [WQX+ 26].
The remainder of this section fixes the primitives of the problem, namely the task stream, the
information available before a call, the pathwise ledger, and the rule that stops service when
spending reaches or crosses the budget.

3.1   Tasks, Models, and Information
We model this problem in a bandit framework. Let M be the finite set of available LLMs and
regard each one as an arm, so that M = |M| is the number of arms. The total number of tasks T
and the total budget B are known in advance, but the contents and diﬀiculty of future tasks are
unknown until they arrive. The tasks q1 , . . . , qT arrive online, one at a time, and are served under
this budget. For each task, the decision maker selects and calls an LLM in an attempt to solve
it. We call the policy that makes these model and retry choices the router. A task can receive
at most H ≥ 1 calls and leaves the stream after its first successful response, after the router
abandons it, or after all H attempts have been used. For bookkeeping, we order the S = T H
potential task–attempt steps lexicographically and index them by s = (t − 1)H + h ∈ [S], where
t ∈ [T ] is a task and h ∈ [H] an attempt. Conversely, (ts , hs ) denotes the task and attempt
associated with step s, with ts = ⌈s/H⌉ and hs = s − (ts − 1)H. Let Gs ∈ {0, 1} indicate
whether step s executes a call. For a first attempt when there is still remaining budget, we
always have Gs = 1. While for a retry, Gs = 1 only if the router chooses to call rather than
abandon and move to next task. For an abandoned or solved task, Gu = 0 for every later step
u > s with tu = ts . And we write as ∈ M for the arm called when Gs = 1. When H = 1, the
problem contains one model choice per task and no retry decision.

State and information. Before step s, the router observes a state xs in a state space X ,
a context summary constructed from known information, including the current prompt, the
failed responses already obtained for task ts , the attempt index hs , and the budget ledger.
Let Fs be the σ-field generated by all states, decisions, outcomes, and ledger values realized
through step s − 1 together with the current state xs , so that Fs is the information available
before the response, and the sequence {Fs }s∈[S] is the pre-outcome filtration. Every pre-call
quantity, including the state, the budget, the decision to call, and each selection score, is Fs -
measurable, whereas the response and its cost are not. We take xs to be suﬀicient for the
response, in the sense that the solve probability and the response-length distribution of a called
arm depend on the past only through xs , so the conditional means and survival probabilities
used in Section 4 are functions of (a, xs ). The router’s estimators, shadow price, and pacing
multiplier, by contrast, depend on the whole realized path, so decisions are required to be Fs -
measurable rather than functions of xs alone. The policy later uses two deterministic views

4     Methodology
We develop ADP-HT, an online policy for LLM routing, which is motivated by the bid-price
principle of online resource allocation. Consider a budgeted bandit problem. In each round, the
decision maker selects an arm, receives a reward r, and consumes c units of a limited resource.
Resource spent on the current pull is no longer available in later rounds. Then each arm’s
value can be measured by the reward it produces minus the opportunity cost of the resource it
consumes, which can be expressed as
                                                                                                                        Q = r − λc,                                                      (4)


                                                                                                                                  8
where λ is the shadow price, the opportunity cost of consuming one unit of budget. And Q can
be viewed as the net value of the action, so the decision maker should select the arm with the
highest Q.
    However, this score is not directly available in LLM routing. In this setting, each arm
corresponds to a candidate LLM. The reward r records whether its call solves the current task,
with r = 1 for success and r = 0 otherwise, and c is the budget charged for the call, but the
router does not know either exactly before a call is made. For example, even when the prompt
length and token prices are known, the number of output tokens is unknown until the response
is generated, and the exact charge cannot be determined in advance. So it has to learn the
solve probability from selected calls and estimate the cost before the response returns. Because
one long response can exhaust the remaining budget, the cost term should take this risk into
account. The shadow price should also track realized spending adaptively to better utilize the
remaining budget. And for tasks that fail on the first attempt, the router needs to decide
whether to retry the same task or preserve the budget for later tasks. The rest of this section
builds ADP-HT based on the score (4) with modifications for these challenges.
    ADP-HT supplies these quantities in four modules designed for LLM routing. For the reward
(see Section 4.1), a hybrid learner combines a shared task-diﬀiculty component with a model-
specific adjustment and reports an upper confidence bound (UCB) on the solve probability.
For the cost (see Section 4.2), we utilize a K-bit prediction of the response length to construct
a one-sided pre-call cost bound that covers the tail of the response-length distribution. It is
diﬀicult to predict the exact output tokens for a LLM, but it is much easier to predict whether
the response will be short, medium, or long. This motivates the K-bit threshold-survival heads,
which can help the router predict more reliably with a loss of resolution, introducing a trade-off
between resolution and precision. The shadow price is controlled by a dual-pace mechanism (see
Section 4.3). Its fast component updates the price directly from the latest call’s charge, while
its slow component updates a pacing multiplier from an exponential moving average of recent
charges. These components adjust the cost penalty and the current task’s spending reference
in response to realized spending and remaining capacity. Finally, the retry gate (see Section
4.4) decides whether a failed task should continue competing for capacity, using the current
pre-call score of the selected candidate and a margin computed after the previous failure. The
margin rises with retry depth and with spend beyond the revised task allowance. The following
subsections describe these modules, and Algorithm 1 gives the full policy.

4.1   Reward Learner
The first term r of the score (4), the reward that a model solves the current task, is provided
by the reward learner. Estimating it separately for every model wastes information because a
diﬀicult prompt tends to be diﬀicult for several models, and a call to any model reveals something
about that common diﬀiculty. Pooling all calls into one predictor creates the opposite problem,
because models have different strengths. The hybrid reward learner therefore combines a shared
task component with a model-specific adjustment.
    The reward feature xrs of step s is a high-dimensional prompt embedding, and the calls
available to each model are limited in online routing. The shared component therefore works on
the projected feature zs = P xrs , where P ∈ Rd0 ×dr is a fixed projection matrix with orthonormal
rows and d0 ≪ dr , so the pooled block is estimated in a much smaller space. An unregularized
fit would still be sensitive to directions that the executed calls sample poorly, so we estimate
both components jointly under a ridge penalty, which limits the influence of those directions
[Has20].
    Every executed call updates the shared suﬀicient statistics and the local suﬀicient statistics
of the selected model. The fit returns a shared coeﬀicient β̂s ∈ Rd0 and a coeﬀicient θ̂s,a ∈ Rdr
for each model a.

4.2   Cost Learner
The term c of (4) is supplied by the cost learner. For a call to LLM a, the cost consists of two
parts, an input charge and an output charge, with known per-token prices ρin             out
                                                                                 a and ρa . The
input side of the charge is already known at admission (the prompt), so the quantity to forecast
is the response length, for which the natural device is a point estimate. Such an estimate
is unreliable, because a prompt does not determine a length. Even holding the model and
the decoding configuration fixed, repeated calls on the same prompt trace out a heavy-tailed
distribution of output lengths rather than one deterministic value [WQX+ 26], so the estimate
reports the center of a distribution whose spread is precisely what matters. The consequences
of prediction error are asymmetric. Overprediction lowers a model’s selection score and can
discourage a retry that the budget could have afforded. Underprediction can instead favor a
response whose realized charge crosses the budget and ends service for every later task. The
selection score therefore needs a statement about the tail of the length distribution rather than
only its center.
     The K-bit mechanism takes the place of that point estimate. The resolution parameter K
determines the number of response-length intervals, J = 2K . Rather than predict precisely how
many tokens a response will contain, the router first discretizes the range of possible lengths
into J intervals, then asks at each interval boundary whether the response will run past it, and
reports the upper edge of an interval as its final prediction. This can lower the diﬀiculty of the
prediction, and the reason is easy to see intuitively. At K = 1, for instance, the router predicts
only whether a response will be short or long. That single bit can be easier to get right than
an exact point estimate, and it also helps control the risk that a misprediction lets an overlong
response exhaust the remaining budget. The price of the lost resolution is just as visible. A
coarse grid limits how precisely the learner can locate the target response-length threshold. A
larger K supplies more thresholds, and every call updates all heads of its selected model. The
trade-off comes from requiring confidence across more estimates, which increases the estimation
allowance at a fixed sample size under the stated confidence calibration. The analysis following
Algorithm 1 quantifies this trade-off between resolution and precision.

4.3    Dual-Pace Controller
The dual-pace controller sets the shadow price λs in (4) and the budget references used to
evaluate each call. At decision step s, the remaining budget Rs needs to support the Ns tasks
remaining in the stream, including the current task. Since the diﬀiculty of future tasks is
unknown, we use the equal share b̄s = Rs /Ns as an initial spending reference. A strict per-task
cap at this share can be too restrictive. If inexpensive tasks repeatedly leave part of their share
unused, the share available to later tasks increases. A strict cap can therefore limit spending on
earlier tasks even when budget is left unused at the end. Allowing unrestricted spending creates
the opposite risk, because early tasks may exhaust the budget before later tasks are served.
    ADP-HT uses the dual-pace controller to manage this tradeoff. A pacing multiplier ϕs
adjusts the spending reference for the current task based on recent average call costs. A shadow
price λs adjusts the cost penalty in model selection based on the cost of the latest call. The
former responds to the recent spending pattern, while the latter responds directly to the latest
call.

    We next examine how the value of tail protection varies with the number of remaining
tasks. Suppose we route T identical tasks, with one call per task and total budget B, using two
models with known distributions. The predictable model P has deterministic cost c and success
probability u. The risky model R has success probability v > u. It costs ℓ with probability
1 − ε and d ≥ B with probability ε, where ℓ < c < d. A response that reaches or crosses the
budget is still completed and, if successful, counted before service stops.


                                                    14
    It is easy to construct an instance in which every fixed mean-cost score prefers R. For
example, take (ℓ, c, d, ε, u, v, B) = (1, 4, 9, 0.2, 0.75, 1, 7.5). Then R has both a higher success
probability and a lower mean cost. Thus, for every λ ≥ 0, the score (4) prefers R.
    However, the budget stopping rule changes this conclusion because a high-cost call to R
may end future service. With few tasks remaining, there is little future service to lose, so R is
better. As more tasks remain, avoiding termination protects enough future service to justify
choosing P . With many tasks remaining, however, the extra cost of P uses budget that could
pay for several cheap calls to R, so R becomes better again.
    This example shows how the number of remaining tasks changes the optimal first choice.
Even when R has both a higher success probability and a lower mean cost, the optimal first
choice moves from R to P and then back to R as the number of remaining tasks increases.
A fixed score whose predictions do not change with the number of remaining tasks cannot
reproduce these changes. ADP-HT uses a state-dependent cost penalty: the effective price
λs /btask
     s    on the cost bound in (15a) responds to realized spending and the remaining budget per
unfinished task. The example yields the following structural insight.

Remark 1. For a given budget, the value of tail protection can rise and fall as the number of
remaining tasks increases. With few remaining tasks, there is little future service to protect.
With many remaining tasks, the extra cost of tail protection displaces cheap risky calls. The
optimal first choice can therefore be risky, then predictable, then risky again, which should be
adaptively reflected in the model score.

    This analysis motivates a state-dependent cost penalty. EC.5 gives the formal results and

particular, fixed initialization cannot reproduce both backlog reversals.


5     Numerical Experiments
We conduct three experiments under the operating rules of Section 3. Experiment 1 (Section 5.1)
compares the implemented policies on live MATH500 [HBK+ 21] and GPQA [RHS+ 24a] streams,
with retries. It examines performance across budgets and sensitivity to the resolution parame-
ter K, the retry gate, and pacing. Experiment 2 evaluates adapted configurations on a public
benchmark (Section 5.2), and Experiment 3 compares test-time allocation with a commercial
router provided by OpenRouter (Section 5.3). We also conduct experiments in EC.3 and EC.4
to examine the K-bit mechanism through cost predictor ablations and assess how closely ADP-
HT approaches the dynamic programming optimum. All experiments run on a MacBook Air
with an Apple M4 chip and 16GB of RAM.
    We compare ADP-HT against four implemented policies from the multi-LLM routing liter-
ature and a deployed commercial router. Budget-aware (BA) and Position-aware (PA) LinUCB
[PDL+ 26] are budget-aware contextual bandit routers. ParetoBandit [TM26] is ported to the
finite stream with its primal–dual reward–cost rule intact, and Greedy LinUCB always calls the
arm with the highest UCB, standing in for a budget-unaware online router.

5.1   Experiment 1: Routing and Retries Under a Binding Budget
We use a 20%/80% stratified split of each dataset into warm and test streams, with five shared-
warm-start replicates per dataset. The warm stream is used to construct the warm-up data to
initialize the predictors, which is excluded from budget accounting. And the budget is for the
test stream only. A task may receive at most H = 4 calls and leaves the stream after its first
verifier-confirmed success, and the outcome is the number of distinct test tasks solved before
service ends. The MATH500 budget grid is B ∈ {0.025, 0.05, 0.075, 0.125, 0.30, 0.45, 0.60}
USD, to which GPQA adds 0.80 and 1.00. The tightest budgets cover only a small fraction

6     Conclusion
We study LLM routing as an online allocation problem in which a call’s cost is learned only
after the budget has been committed. Under a halt-after-crossing budget, an admission decision


                                                                                                       19
affects both the current task and every later task the remaining budget could have served, so
controlling spending in expectation does not control that exposure. We formulate the prob-
lem as K-bit Pathwise Contextual Bandits with Knapsacks. Exact charges accumulate in the
ledger, while the cost learner observes only which of 2K response-length intervals a call landed
in. ADP-HT turns those labels into a one-sided pre-call cost bound and prices it by the current
shadow value of capacity, so one score governs both model choice and retries. The analy-
sis gives a conditional K-bit trade-off for the implemented quantile mesh. More bits reduce
core discretization but increase the allowance for simultaneous estimation, with an additional
term for edge-placement error. A matching construction establishes the tightness of the two
K-dependent terms under the stated confidence calibration. This bound motivates choosing
resolution to match the available sample size. Under the protocols we report, the gains con-
centrate where the budget determines which tasks are reached. At the tightest tested budgets
ADP-HT completes more than twice as many MATH500 and GPQA tasks as the best imple-
mented comparator, leads ten of fourteen RouterBench budgets, and outperforms a deployed
commercial router at the three lowest test budgets.
    Future work. Several directions remain open. First, extending pathwise budget control
to open-ended work such as writing, analysis, and code review would require modeling the
uncertainty of a learned judge and its rubric-based evaluations. The resolution–estimation
trade-off could then appear in the grading mesh as well as the length mesh, which makes that
setting a natural test of how far the mechanism travels. Second, a joint treatment of selection,
pricing, pacing, and retries would bring the dual path inside the guarantee and deliver an end-to-
end pathwise statement. Lower bounds over routing policies would clarify how much additional
service can be gained from finer cost information. Third, sharper response-length prediction is
an immediately practical lever. Any predictor that localizes the conditional tail more tightly
shrinks the estimation radius at a fixed resolution, so the same mesh admits a tighter bound,
and the mechanism composes with advances made elsewhere. Fourth, an adaptive mesh that
re-places its edges and reset K as the workload drifts, would make the resolution parameter K
and edge positions a control component that the operator can tune during service rather than
a constant fixed at warm start.

We prove (17) and derive its model-selection implication. We then give statistical conditions
