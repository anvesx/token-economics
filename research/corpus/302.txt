                                            multi-step interactions, long and noisy observations incur substantial context overhead and

                                        1     Introduction
                                        Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly use external tools to acquire information and
                                        solve multi-step tasks Yang et al. (2024); Hong et al. (2025); Wang et al. (2023), with web search as a
                                        representative example Schick et al. (2023); Li et al. (2025a); He et al. (2024); Li et al. (2026). Under
                                        the widely adopted ReAct Yao et al. (2022) paradigm, an agent first reasons over the task and its
                                        current context to select a tool call, then adds the returned observation to its context to take the
                                        next action. For example, after querying a search engine, a web-search agent appends the returned
                                        search results to its context and uses them to decide whether to perform another search or provide
                                        a final answer Li et al. (2025a).
                                        However, appending raw tool observations leads to rapid context growth over successive reasoning
                                        steps, introducing two drawbacks. First, increased computational overhead. As shown in Figure 1
                                        (left), tool observations account for a large and growing share of the context, reaching 96.7% at
                                        Step 4 and leading to substantially higher token usage and computational cost Xiao et al. (2026);
                                        Jiang et al. (2023). Second, degraded task performance. In Figure 1 (right), identifying the name
                                        Sébastien Buemi suffices to guide the next search for his birth year, while extensive race details are
                                        irrelevant to this decision. Such redundant content consumes context and can distract the model
                                        from key evidence, impairing reasoning quality Liu et al. (2024); Levy et al. (2024); Shi et al. (2023).
                                        A natural solution is to compress the agent’s context. Existing methods mainly use token pruning or
                                        text summarization. Token pruning uses a model to identify and remove less informative tokens Li
                                        et al. (2023); Jiang et al. (2023); Pan et al. (2024); Jiang et al. (2024). Text summarization uses an
                                        auxiliary LLM to condense tool outputs or interaction histories into shorter summaries Wang et al.
                                        (2026); Xu et al. (2023). However, such text-level compression has a fundamental flaw: because

A closer look at tool–agent interactions raises a fundamental question: must tool outputs be
expressed in textual tokens? Since these outputs primarily support an agent’s subsequent reasoning
and actions, tool–agent interaction need not rely on explicit textual tokens. This observation
motivates us to reconsider how tool information is represented. Compared with discrete text tokens,
continuous latent tokens (i.e., high-dimensional vectors in the embedding space) offer a more
flexible alternative. These latent tokens can encode rich information compactly under a limited
token budget, while their differentiability enables direct optimization for downstream objectives.
Recent studies Yu et al. (2026); Chen et al. (2026b) have explored latent tokens in reasoning Hao
et al. (2024); Cheng & Van Durme (2024); Xu et al. (2025); Deng et al. (2023); Anonymous (2026),
agents’ communication Ye et al. (2026); Zou et al. (2025); Du et al. (2026); Jin et al. (2026), and
memory Zhang et al. (2025a; 2026); Wang et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2025b). Together, these studies
suggest that models can directly process and exchange information through latent tokens, opening
up new possibilities for information transfer between tools and agents.
Motivated by this analysis, we propose Coca-Cola (Context Compression via Continuous Latent
Tokens), a latent context compression model for LLM agents. Conditioned on the task instruction
and the agent’s current-step reasoning trajectory, Coca-Cola compresses long, noisy tool observations
into a few latent tokens that retain information critical to subsequent decisions. These latent tokens
are mapped into the agent’s input embedding space, enabling it to use the compressed information
for further reasoning and actions.
Although latent tokens enable efficient information compression, applying them to compress tool
observations for agents introduces two key challenges. The first is to make compressed representa-
tions effectively usable by the agent. The second is to preserve decision-critical information under
a limited representation budget. To address the first challenge, we design a context-aware encoder
with a latent adapter that maps compressed representations into the target agent’s input embedding
space. This allows the agent to directly use these latent tokens for subsequent reasoning and actions.
For the second challenge, we propose an action-preserving training method. Specifically, we align
the agent’s next-action distributions under full and compressed tool observations. This trains the
compression model to minimize its impact on the agent’s next-action behavior, thereby preserving
action-critical information.
We evaluate Coca-Cola across two model configurations and four web-search QA benchmarks.
Compared to uncompressed agents, Coca-Cola reduces total token consumption by 70.01%–89.24%
and end-to-end runtime by 24.45%–55.97%, demonstrating substantial efficiency gains. Despite
aggressive compression, Coca-Cola consistently outperforms the uncompressed agent in both EM

         efficiency while consistently improving task performance.

2     Related Work
2.1   LLM-Based Agents
LLM-based agents typically follow the ReAct paradigm Yao et al. (2022), alternating between
reasoning and tool use to accomplish multi-step tasks. Formally, given a task instruction 𝑞, an
agent parameterized by 𝜓 generates a reasoning step 𝑟𝑡 and issues a tool call 𝑎𝑡 at step 𝑡, receiving
a tool observation 𝑜𝑡 . Let ℎ𝑡 = (𝑟1 , 𝑎1 , 𝑜1 , … , 𝑟𝑡 , 𝑎𝑡 ) denote the trajectory prefix before receiving
𝑜𝑡 , comprising previous reasoning steps, tool calls, and observations. The agent appends 𝑜𝑡 to its
context and continues reasoning to select the next tool call or produce a final answer:
                                           𝑎𝑡+1 ∼ 𝑝𝜓 (⋅ ∣ 𝑞, ℎ𝑡 , 𝑜𝑡 ).                                  (1)

As tool interactions proceed, the agent accumulates previous reasoning steps, tool calls, and their
observations in its context. Without compression or truncation, the context length after the 𝑡-th
tool call can be approximated as
                                                     𝑡
                                     𝐾𝑡 ≈ |𝑞| + ∑ (|𝑟𝑖 | + |𝑎𝑖 | + |𝑜𝑖 |) .                              (2)
                                                    𝑖=1

where | ⋅ | denotes the token count and 𝐾𝑡 is the total context length, excluding fixed prompt and
formatting overhead. Since tool calls often return lengthy search results and document snippets,
tool observations accumulate over successive interactions and can eventually dominate the agent’s
context, incurring substantial context overhead.

2.2   Textual Compression for Tool-Augmented Agents
To reduce the context overhead of tool interactions, existing methods primarily rely on token
pruning and text summarization Wang et al. (2026); Xiao et al. (2026); Chen et al. (2026a); Shi et al.
(2025). Formally, given the tool observation 𝑜𝑡 at step 𝑡, text-based compression methods transform
it into a shorter sequence of discrete tokens using a compression function 𝑔𝜂 :
                                   𝑜̂ 𝑡 = 𝑔𝜂 (𝑞, ℎ𝑡 , 𝑜𝑡 ) ∈  𝓁𝑡 ,   𝓁𝑡 < |𝑜𝑡 |,                        (3)
where  denotes the vocabulary, 𝓁𝑡 is the compressed sequence length. The agent then replaces the
original observation 𝑜𝑡 with its compressed counterpart 𝑜̂ 𝑡 to select the next action:
                                           𝑎𝑡+1 ∼ 𝑝𝜓 (⋅ ∣ 𝑞, ℎ𝑡 , 𝑜̂ 𝑡 ).                                (4)

Token-pruning methods selectively remove less informative tokens. LLMLingua estimates token
informativeness using language models (Jiang et al., 2023), while LLMLingua-2 formulates compres-
sion as a token classification task (Pan et al., 2024). LongLLMLingua further improves the density
of task-relevant information in long-context prompts (Jiang et al., 2024).
Text summarization instead employs auxiliary language models to condense tool outputs or in-
teraction histories into shorter natural-language summaries. RECOMP generates query-relevant
summaries of retrieved documents (Xu et al., 2023), while Search-o1 extracts useful information
from search results through its Reason-in-Documents module (Li et al., 2025a; Jin et al., 2025). For

long-horizon agents, ACON jointly compresses tool observations and interaction histories, refining
compression instructions based on agent failures (Kang et al., 2025).
Despite their different strategies, these methods retain information in the form of discrete text
tokens. In contrast, Coca-Cola compresses tool observations into continuous latent representations,
moving beyond text-based compression.

2.3   Latent Tokens for Agents
In recent years, continuous latent tokens have been explored for reasoning, communication, and
memory in large language agents. For latent reasoning, Coconut (Hao et al., 2024), CCoT (Cheng
& Van Durme, 2024), and SoftCoT (Xu et al., 2025) explore reasoning in continuous latent spaces,
reducing reliance on explicit natural-language reasoning processes. For agent communication,
KVComm (Ye et al., 2026) and LatentMAS (Zou et al., 2025) investigate information exchange
between agents through hidden states or KV caches. For memory, MemGen (Zhang et al., 2025a) and
NextMem (Zhang et al., 2026) explore storing and utilizing historical information through continuous
latent tokens. These studies demonstrate that latent tokens can support both internal information
processing and inter-model communication, offering new possibilities beyond traditional text-based
interactions.
Another line of research employs latent tokens to compress long textual contexts Rau et al. (2024);
Cheng et al. (2024); Li et al. (2025b); Chevalier et al. (2023); Mu et al. (2023); Ge et al. (2023). Gist
Tokens (Mu et al., 2023) learns special compression tokens to encode long prompts into compact
representations; AutoCompressor (Chevalier et al., 2023) compresses long texts into continuous
summary vectors; and 500xCompressor (Li et al., 2025b) explores context compression at extremely
high compression ratios. Among these approaches, ICAE (Ge et al., 2023), which is closely related to
our work, employs an autoencoder-based architecture to compress long texts into a small number
of continuous vectors and trains a language model to utilize these latent tokens for downstream
tasks.
However, existing latent context compression methods primarily focus on preserving or recon-
structing the original textual information. In contrast, Coca-Cola directly optimizes for the agent’s
subsequent decisions, preserving decision-critical information while discarding redundant content.

3     Method
3.1   Overview
As shown in Figure 2, we propose Coca-Cola, a continuous latent context compression model for
LLM agents. Unlike traditional text-based compression methods, Coca-Cola compresses lengthy
tool observations into a small number of latent tokens to reduce the context overhead of tool
interactions. We first theoretically analyze the information capacity of latent tokens, providing
theoretical motivation for compressing tool observations into continuous vectors (Section 3.2).
Specifically, after each tool call, Coca-Cola takes the task instruction, the agent’s current-step
reasoning trajectory, and the tool observation as input. A lightweight encoder processes these inputs,
while a latent adapter maps the resulting latent tokens into the target agent’s input embedding
space (Section 3.3). To preserve decision-critical information under a limited representation budget,
we further introduce Action-Preserving Training, which trains the compressor by aligning the
agent’s next-action distributions under full and compressed observations(Section 3.4). During
inference, the trained compressor replaces raw tool observations with latent tokens after each tool
call, allowing the agent to perform multi-step interactions with a more compact context (Section
3.5).

3.2   Theoretical Analysis
We analyze the potential advantage of latent tokens through their finite-precision codebook capacity.
Here, capacity is defined as the logarithm of the number of distinguishable codewords, with the
context shared by the encoder and the agent held fixed.

For a model with vocabulary size ||, each text token is selected from || symbols. A sequence of 𝑛
tokens therefore admits at most ||𝑛 distinct sequences, giving a maximum codebook capacity of
                                        text (𝑛) = 𝑛 log2 ||.                                 (5)

A latent token can instead encode information jointly across multiple numerical coordinates within
a single input position. Assume each 𝑑-dimensional latent token has 𝑑eff ≤ 𝑑 effective coordinates,
each admitting 𝐵 distinguishable values at finite precision, and that all coordinate combinations
across representations are realizable. Under these assumptions, 𝑚 representations have a codebook
capacity of
                                       latent (𝑚) = 𝑚𝑑eff log2 𝐵.                              (6)
Thus, when 𝑑eff log2 𝐵 > log2 ||, continuous latent representations can provide a larger codebook
capacity under the same input-position budget, i.e., 𝑚 = 𝑛. This potential comes from joint encoding
across multiple coordinates, whereas each text position remains restricted to a symbol from a fixed
vocabulary. This analysis motivates continuous compression of tool observations from a capacity
perspective; the information usable in practice still depends on the compressor’s encoding ability
and the agent’s ability to use the representations.

3.3          Model Architecture
Building on the above theoretical analysis, we propose Coca-Cola, a continuous latent context
compression model for LLM agents. Coca-Cola encodes tool observations into a small number of
latent tokens and maps them into the target agent’s input embedding space. To make these latent
tokens directly usable by the agent, we design a compression model consisting of a context-aware
encoder and a latent adapter, as illustrated in Figure 2 (left).
Context-Aware Encoder. Our analysis of tool-use trajectories shows that a tool observation is not
only relevant to the observation itself, but also depends on the task instruction and the agent’s
current reasoning state. To this end, we employ a small language model 𝐶𝜃 as the context-aware
encoder, which jointly encodes the task instruction, current-step reasoning trajectory, and tool
observation. Specifically, given the task instruction 𝑞, current-step reasoning trajectory prefix 𝑟𝑡 ,
and tool observation 𝑜𝑡 , the model produces context-conditioned hidden states:
                                         𝐻𝑡 = 𝐶𝜃 (𝑞, 𝑟𝑡 , 𝑜𝑡 ).                                  (7)

Latent Adapter. Since the context-aware encoder and the target agent operate in different repre-
sentation spaces, the encoded hidden states cannot be directly consumed by the agent. To bridge
this gap, we introduce a latent adapter 𝐴𝜙 that transforms the hidden states into a fixed number of
latent tokens and maps them into the target agent’s input embedding space:
                                       𝑧𝑡 = 𝐴𝜙 (𝐻𝑡 ) ∈ ℝ𝑚×𝑑 ,                                 (8)
where 𝑚 is the number of latent tokens and 𝑑 is the target agent’s input embedding dimension.
The resulting latent tokens are then directly inserted into the agent’s input embedding sequence,
delimited by special boundary markers:
                   𝑋𝑡 = [𝐸𝜓 (𝑞, ℎ𝑡 ); 𝐸𝜓 (⟨latent_obs⟩); 𝑧𝑡 ; 𝐸𝜓 (⟨/latent_obs⟩)].           (9)
Here, 𝐸𝜓 denotes the target agent’s input embedding function. This design provides a continuous
interface between tool observations and the target agent.

3.4   Action-Preserving Training
Although latent tokens can encode tool observations compactly, preserving decision-critical infor-
mation under a limited representation budget remains challenging. To address this challenge, we
design a training method that aligns the agent’s next-action distributions under full and compressed
observations, encouraging the compression model to retain information critical to next-action
selection.
Specifically, given the task instruction 𝑞, trajectory prefix ℎ𝑡 , and tool observation 𝑜𝑡 , we feed the
full observation 𝑜𝑡 and its compressed latent tokens 𝑧𝑡 separately into the same frozen target agent.
The resulting next-action distributions are defined as
                                         𝑝full = 𝑝𝜓 (⋅ ∣ 𝑞, ℎ𝑡 , 𝑜𝑡 ),
                                                                                                    (10)
                                       𝑝latent = 𝑝𝜓 (⋅ ∣ 𝑞, ℎ𝑡 , 𝑧𝑡 ).
Our goal is to align these distributions, minimizing the impact of compression on the agent’s
subsequent decisions.
To this end, we jointly optimize an action-distribution alignment loss and an action-level supervision
loss:
                           = 𝜆KL 𝐷KL (𝑝full ∥ 𝑝latent ) + 𝜆CE CE(𝑦𝑡+1 , 𝑝latent ),                (11)
where 𝑦𝑡+1 denotes the next-action token recorded in the collected trajectory, and 𝜆KL and 𝜆CE
control the respective loss weights. The KL divergence aligns the action distributions under full
and compressed observations, while the cross-entropy loss further supervises the predictions
using recorded actions. Together, these objectives encourage the compression model to prioritize
information relevant to subsequent decisions.
Since latent tokens are continuous and differentiable, gradients can propagate through the frozen
target agent to optimize the compression model:
                                                           ⊤
                                                 𝜕𝑧𝑡
                                      ∇𝜃,𝜙  =           ∇ .                                     (12)
                                             ( 𝜕(𝜃, 𝜙) ) 𝑧𝑡
During training, we keep the target agent frozen and update only the LoRA parameters of the
context-aware encoder and the parameters of the latent adapter. By directly aligning the agent’s
action distributions under full and compressed observations, Coca-Cola learns action-sufficient latent
tokens without reconstructing the original tool observations.

3.5   Inference
At inference, Coca-Cola replaces each raw tool observation with latent tokens. Given the task
instruction 𝑞, current-step trajectory prefix 𝑟𝑡 , and tool observation 𝑜𝑡 , the trained compression
model produces
                                        𝑧𝑡 = 𝐴𝜙 (𝐶𝜃 (𝑞, 𝑟𝑡 , 𝑜𝑡 )) .                                 (13)
The agent then conditions on 𝑧𝑡 , rather than the raw observation, to generate its next action:
                                        𝑎𝑡+1 ∼ 𝑝𝜓 (⋅ ∣ 𝑞, ℎ𝑡 , 𝑧𝑡 ).                                (14)
For multi-step tool use, Coca-Cola repeats this process after every tool call. By replacing tool outputs
with a small number of latent tokens, Coca-Cola reduces context growth while retaining information
needed for subsequent decisions.

RQ1 (Efficiency): How much computational overhead can Coca-Cola reduce in terms of token

We measure token consumption, compression rates, and end-to-end runtime across different model

RQ2 (Task Performance): Can Coca-Cola preserve or improve agent task performance while

We compare EM and F1 scores against uncompressed agents and existing compression methods to

We investigate how the number of latent tokens and different contextual inputs affect compression

4.2   Agent Setting and Model Configurations
We evaluate Coca-Cola in a web-search agent setting, as web search is one of the most common
tool-use scenarios for LLM agents Li et al. (2025a); Jin et al. (2025), and its returned results are
typically lengthy and highly redundant (Figure 1), making it a natural testbed for observation
compression. Notably, Coca-Cola involves no search-specific design: the compressor conditions only
on the task instruction, the current reasoning trajectory, and the raw tool observation, and can thus
be applied to other tools without architectural modification.
To evaluate Coca-Cola across different model configurations, we use two model pairs: Qwen3.5-35B-
A3B with Qwen3.5-4B Qwen Team (2026), and GLM-4.7-Flash with GLM-Z1-9B GLM Team et al.
(2025); Team GLM et al. (2024).

4.3   Baselines
We compare Coca-Cola against four representative approaches:
Vanilla Model. An uncompressed agent that directly appends raw tool observations to its context.



                                                            8
Tsinghua AI Agent Group



LongLLMLingua. A token-pruning method that compresses context by selectively removing less
informative tokens Jiang et al. (2024).
LLMSummary. A text summarization approach that uses an auxiliary language model to summarize
tool observations into shorter natural-language text Li et al. (2025a).
ICAE. ICAE Ge et al. (2023) learns continuous latent tokens through text reconstruction. Since
ICAE was not originally designed for agent tool observations, we adapt its training objective to
our setting. Specifically, we use the same compression architecture as Coca-Cola but replace the
action-preserving training objective with a text-reconstruction objective, training the model to
recover the original tool observations from compressed latent tokens.

4.4   Benchmarks
We evaluate on four open-domain question answering benchmarks: Natural Questions
(NQ) Kwiatkowski et al. (2019), TriviaQA Joshi et al. (2017), 2WikiMultiHopQA (2Wiki) Ho
et al. (2020), and HotpotQA Yang et al. (2018).
NQ and TriviaQA primarily assess the agent’s ability to retrieve and utilize factual evidence, while
2Wiki and HotpotQA require multi-hop reasoning across multiple pieces of evidence. Together,
these benchmarks evaluate whether latent compression preserves critical information under varying
reasoning complexity.

5.1   RQ1: How much computational overhead can Coca-Cola reduce?
In this RQ, we evaluate the efficiency gains of Coca-Cola in terms of token consumption and end-
to-end execution time across different model configurations and QA benchmarks. The results are
presented in Tables 1 and 2.
Coca-Cola substantially reduces tool-context overhead. Compared with the Vanilla Model, which
directly incorporates full tool observations into the agent’s context, Coca-Cola consistently reduces
both input and output token consumption across all evaluated datasets. For instance, on NQ with the
Qwen models, the average number of input tokens decreases from 14,362.12 to 1,766.53, representing
an 87.70% reduction. Similarly, on 2Wiki with the GLM models, input token consumption drops
from 31,693.47 to 3,003.38, corresponding to a 90.52% reduction. These results demonstrate that
replacing noisy tool observations with compact latent tokens effectively mitigates context growth
while reducing subsequent generation overhead.
Coca-Cola achieves high compression rates across all settings. Coca-Cola reduces total token
consumption by 70.01%–89.24% and achieves the highest compression rate in five of the eight
model–dataset settings. Compared with ICAE, which shares the same architecture but uses a
reconstruction objective, Coca-Cola achieves a higher compression rate in all eight settings; for
example, on 2Wiki with the GLM models, it reaches 89.24% versus 74.12% for ICAE. LLMSummary

5.2   RQ2: Can Coca-Cola improve agent task performance while reducing tool-token overhead?
In this RQ, we evaluate whether Coca-Cola can preserve or improve agent performance while
reducing tool-context overhead, using Exact Match (EM) and F1. The results are presented in
Table 3.
High compression rates do not compromise task performance, but can even lead to improvements.
Compared with the Vanilla Model, Coca-Cola consistently achieves higher EM and F1 across all
evaluated settings. On TriviaQA with Qwen, Coca-Cola improves F1 from 62.87% to 67.07% while
achieving an 83.68% compression rate. Similarly, on 2Wiki with GLM, F1 increases from 31.29% to
36.20% at an 89.24% compression rate. These results suggest that filtering redundant and irrelevant
tool information may alleviate attention dilution caused by lengthy contexts, allowing agents to
focus more effectively on task-relevant evidence.
Coca-Cola outperforms existing compression methods in most settings. Coca-Cola achieves the
highest score in 13 of the 16 dataset–metric pairs, and the highest EM and F1 simultaneously in six
of the eight model–dataset combinations, including all four with the GLM models. For example,
on TriviaQA with GLM, Coca-Cola achieves an F1 of 59.41%, surpassing the strongest baseline,
LLMSummary (49.29%). Similarly, on HotpotQA with Qwen, Coca-Cola achieves an F1 of 33.75%,
compared with 31.21% for the strongest baseline, ICAE. The only exceptions occur with the Qwen
models, where ICAE achieves higher EM and F1 on TriviaQA and LongLLMLingua achieves a
higher F1 on 2Wiki; however, these baselines compress tool observations considerably less than
Coca-Cola (66.92% vs. 83.68% and 41.96% vs. 70.01%, respectively). Unlike text-based or generic latent
compression, Coca-Cola prioritizes decision-relevant information, even under high compression
rates.

5.4   RQ4: How does Coca-Cola support agent decision-making through latent tokens?
Figure 4 presents a representative case from HotpotQA. The search results contain both information
linking Jens Risom to Mid-Century Modern design and key evidence that he introduced Scandinavian
design to America. The Vanilla Model fails to distinguish between these two pieces of information
and produces an incorrect answer even after an additional search. In contrast, Coca-Cola identifies
the evidence directly relevant to the question and arrives at the correct answer with fewer searches
and lower context overhead.

6     Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce Coca-Cola, a continuous latent context compression model for compacting
noisy tool observations in LLM agents. Unlike conventional token pruning and summarization
methods, Coca-Cola encodes tool outputs into a small number of task-conditioned latent tokens
that are directly injected into the agent. Through our action-preserving training, we learn a
compression model that preserves decision-critical information rather than reconstructing the
original observations. Experiments on web-search tasks demonstrate that Coca-Cola substantially
reduces context overhead while consistently improving task performance.
More fundamentally, we challenge a prevailing assumption in LLM agents: interaction between tools
and agents need not rely on explicit text tokens. Instead, tools only need to convey information
sufficient for subsequent decisions through compact, latent tokens. This perspective opens a
promising direction for building more efficient and scalable long-horizon agents.

Appendix
A     Implementation Details
A.1   Training Configuration
We construct the compression dataset from 4,188 distinct tasks, containing 4,363 tool observation–
action pairs. We reserve 10% of the pairs for validation, yielding 3,927 training pairs and 436
validation pairs.
The target agent remains frozen throughout training. We update only the LoRA parameters of the
context-aware model and the parameters of the latent adapter. The LoRA rank, scaling factor, and
dropout rate are set to 16, 32, and 0.05, respectively.
We use paged 8-bit AdamW as the optimizer, with initial learning rates of 1.5 × 10−4 for both the
LoRA parameters and the latent adapter. The learning rate decays linearly to 5 × 10−5 . The weight
decay is set to 0. The loss weights 𝜆KL and 𝜆CE are set to 0.3 and 1.0, respectively. The KL loss
weight is warmed up over the first 20% of training steps.

A.2   Inference Configuration
During inference, each tool observation is compressed into 128 continuous latent tokens and directly
injected into the target agent’s input embedding sequence.
For web search, we retrieve up to 5 results per tool call and allow a maximum of 5 tool calls per task.

A.3   Hardware and Software
All experiments are conducted on 4 NVIDIA A100-SXM4 GPUs, each with 80 GB of memory.

Training. For Coca-Cola, the frozen target agent is distributed across 3 GPUs, while the context-
aware encoder and the latent adapter are placed on the remaining GPU.

Inference. All inference results, including the runtimes reported in Tables 1 and 2, are obtained
with the target agent running on a single GPU in all settings. Where an additional GPU is used, it
hosts only the compression model.
