Abstract
Advances in frontier coding agents have sharply increased the token costs of AI-assisted software
engineering. Their token efficiency is typically evaluated on isolated tasks, whereas real-world
developers use them to continuously build, update, and maintain large-scale projects. This mismatch
motivates us to evaluate agents over sequences of realistic coding tasks, where we find that agents
exhibit recurring environment-dependent token costs that existing methods struggle to reduce. To
minimize these costs, we introduce TokenEconomizer, an external controller that cuts environmentspecific token costs over a sequence of tasks. These cuts come from determining (i) which operational
micro-skills to inject, (ii) which persistently irrelevant tools to prune, and (iii) when to intervene in
unproductive failure loops. Across Claude Code, Codex, and GitHub Copilot CLI paired with frontier
models, TokenEconomizer reduces the average token cost by 18.4–30.9% with comparable task success.
Savings hold in every repository under every tested agent–model setting and generalize to diverse
domains such as sequential web development and security auditing, where costs fall by 6.4–21.2%
depending on task structure. Our work establishes adaptive black-box cost control as a promising
direction for reducing token costs of coding agents in realistic settings.

1 Introduction
Frontier large language models (LLMs), coupled with agent harnesses that support tool use (Yang et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2024b; 2025a), have become increasingly capable of completing realistic, long-horizon software
engineering tasks (Jimenez et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2025; Merrill et al., 2026;
Huang et al., 2026). These components are commonly packaged as commercial products, including Claude
Code, Codex, and GitHub Copilot CLI. These agents are designed primarily for generic task success, but
their increased capability comes at a substantial token cost. As shown in Fig. 1a, the billed context at each
turn includes persistent system and user instructions together with an expanding history of model outputs
and tool interactions, causing earlier content to be charged repeatedly across the trajectory.
Token efficiency has primarily been studied and optimized at the level of individual tasks. Model-level
controls such as lower reasoning effort save tokens at a cost in task success (Table 1), while harness-level
controls such as tool availability and auxiliary instructions typically require manual configuration. More
targeted approaches to trajectory compression, observation filtering, and specialized repository exploration
often require agent-loop modifications or additional trained components (Xiao et al., 2025; Kovács, 2026; Li
et al., 2026b), limiting their plug-and-play deployment. More importantly, task-local optimization overlooks
the continuity of software development, where successive changes occur within an evolving codebase (Xu

We first test whether fixed, externally applied cost-control strategies can deliver consistent token savings.
Our cost profiling covers 60 SWE-Bench tasks across 6 repositories (Jimenez et al., 2024) and evaluates three
representative fixed strategies from popular open-source plugins: command-output compression with Rust
Token Killer (RTK), semantic code navigation with Serena, and structural context injection with Aider’s
repository map. Empirically, we find that no fixed strategy reduces cost consistently: each has both positive
and negative repository-level effects, and their pooled effects are small (Fig. 2). Meanwhile, the default traces
reveal three recurring sources of headroom: operational knowledge is repeatedly rediscovered, most loaded
tools remain unused, and failed trajectories consume disproportionate cost. Taken together, these findings
point to strategies that adapt to each environment from its accumulated traces, rather than fixed strategies
applied uniformly.
Motivated by these findings, we introduce TokenEconomizer, an experience-driven external controller for
coding agents. It treats each agent as a black box: it acts through exposed prompt and tool settings, monitors
execution events, and does not modify model weights or agent-loop code. At its core, a controller applies
general cost-control meta-strategies to continually refine environment-specific policies from completed execution traces. These policies adaptively determine (i) which one-line operational facts about the environment
to activate as micro-skills, (ii) which loaded but irrelevant tools to prune, and (iii) when to intervene in
unproductive failure loops. We evaluate TokenEconomizer with Claude Code, Codex, and GitHub Copilot
CLI, paired with frontier models, on 277 SWE-Bench tasks across 6 repositories, 102 Web-Bench tasks across
5 web-development projects, and 200 tasks from two security-auditing benchmarks. Including all controller
overhead, our method reduces mean per-task cost on SWE-Bench by 18.4–30.9%, while improving Pass@1
over default execution in 4 of 5 agent–model settings. Cost savings hold in every repository under each tested
agent–model setting (Fig. 5a), and TokenEconomizer variants account for every point on the cost–quality
Pareto frontier at or above the default pass rate (Fig. 1b). On Web-Bench and the security benchmarks, it
reduces cost by 16.0–21.2% and 6.4–19.6%, respectively, with Pass@2 and detection F1 remaining comparable
to default execution. Our results show that our external cost-control framework can deliver consistent and
transferable cost reductions across distinct agent harnesses while maintaining comparable task quality.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
1. We formulate coding-agent cost optimization over sequences of tasks and define a standardized protocol
for cumulative end-to-end accounting. Our cost profiling identifies repository-dependent effects of
fixed strategies and three actionable sources of headroom.
2. We introduce TokenEconomizer, an external controller that learns environment-specific policies for
micro-skill activation, tool pruning, and failure-loop intervention.
3. We evaluate TokenEconomizer across three popular coding agents and four benchmarks spanning
579 tasks, demonstrating cost reductions that hold in every setting we measure, with comparable task
success.

2 Cost Profiling and Analysis of Fixed Cost-control Strategies
We conduct a retrospective profiling analysis on 60 SWE-Bench tasks from the verified split, with 10 tasks from
each of 6 repositories, to test whether fixed strategies save cost consistently and whether completed traces
reveal opportunities for adaptive control. As Fig. 1a illustrates, every turn bills a persistent system-and-tool
prefix together with the accumulated interaction history; caching discounts this replayed context but does
not eliminate its cost. Appendix B provides the detailed protocol.

2.1 Repository-specific Effects of Fixed Strategies
We compare the agent’s default execution with three fixed strategies, using the same configuration in every
repository: (1) Rust Token Killer (RTK) rewrites shell commands to compress their outputs; (2) Serena adds a
semantic code-navigation interface; and (3) Aider’s RepoMap injects a structural map of repository symbols.
Although their outputs may depend on the current task, none learns from completed executions or activates
selectively based on prior experience.
Fig. 2 shows that every strategy produces mixed repository-level cost changes. For example, all three reduce
cost on Django but increase it on SymPy; on scikit-learn only RTK helps, while on Sphinx only RepoMap
does. These mixed effects motivate learning repository-specific policies from completed tasks.

Failed trajectories consume disproportionate cost. The 10 failed executions constitute 17% of the tasks
but 32% of total spend (Fig. 3c). A universal turn limit cannot identify them: Matplotlib failures are much
longer than successful runs (45 versus 20 turns), whereas Sphinx failures run only slightly longer than its
successful runs (36 versus 31). In total, 45 trajectories repeat an earlier failure signature, the normalized
first error line of a failing output (Appendix F), and 51% of the 60 tasks’ spend falls after a trajectory’s
first repetition. Repetition alone does not mark failure, however: 36 of the 50 successful runs also repeat a
signature, and more than half of the spend after a repetition falls in runs that go on to succeed. A guard
must therefore separate persistent loops from recoverable ones, for instance by comparing a run’s length
with the repository’s successful runs, and should redirect before it terminates.
Together, these observations motivate three adaptive decisions, which Sec. 3 implements as meta-strategies
whose policies are learned separately within each environment: which operational knowledge to inject,
which persistently irrelevant tools to prune, and when to intervene in an unproductive failure loop.

3 TokenEconomizer
We introduce TokenEconomizer, an external controller that applies three repository-agnostic meta-strategies
to learn repository-specific policies from completed traces. Without modifying the underlying agent, it acts
through the instruction prefix, loaded tools, and live event stream to synthesize and selectively activate
operational micro-skills, prune unnecessary tools, and intervene in unproductive failure loops (Fig. 4).
Throughout, a meta-strategy is one of these three environment-agnostic procedures, a policy is the environmentspecific decision it learns, and a fixed strategy, such as RTK or RepoMap, applies one configuration in every
environment.

Our unit of optimization is a chronological task stream τ1 , . . . , τn within an environment. By default, an agent
runs task τi under an instruction prefix Pi , the harness’s system instructions followed by the description of τi ,
with every tool in the harness’s catalog loaded. When the controller is active, it maintains repository state Si−1 ,
which records the micro-skills and supporting evidence, tool-use history, and turn statistics accumulated
from the completed tasks τ1 , . . . , τi−1 , with S0 empty. Before τi starts, the controller C determines the set of
active policies based on the task description and current state:

(∆Pi , Ui , Bi ) = C(τi , Si−1 ).

(1)

The policies given by C are the set ∆Pi of micro-skills activated at task i, which depends only on Si−1 and is
rendered as a short manual inserted into Pi between the harness instructions and the task description; the
loaded tool set Ui , restricted to catalog tools that remain plausibly useful; and a turn budget Bi that calibrates
when an in-flight failure-loop guard may redirect or terminate the rollout. The agent then runs τi under
these policies, producing a trajectory ξ i , from which the controller updates the repository state to Si . The
controller may also leave the agent’s default configuration in place by activating no micro-skills, keeping the
full tool catalog loaded, or never intervening. Its objective is to minimize mean per-task cost, including its
own calls, while keeping success near default execution (Appendix E, Eq. (6)).

3.1 Meta-strategy I: Experience-grounded Micro-skills
Proposal and evidence collection. The controller periodically condenses a bounded window of completed
trajectories into compact digests of operational evidence, such as command templates, test invocations, edited
file names, and encountered errors. From these digests, an LLM proposer drafts a few candidate micro-skills;
its tokens, like those of the controller’s other LLM calls, are charged to the task that triggers the call. Each
candidate f pairs a simple operational instruction with executable detectors, which are regular expressions
or built-in predicates that test the skill against the actual task trajectory. After each task τi , the controller
runs these detectors on the shell commands and outputs in its trajectory ξ i . A scope detector checks whether
ξ i exercises the skill, that is, whether the situation it addresses arose at all; a skill on reproducing issues, for
example, is exercised only if the agent attempts to write a reproduction script. An exercised task contradicts
the skill if it shows the instruction was wrong, such as an import error for a package the skill says is installed,
5

and otherwise confirms it. A need detector records whether the task needed the skill: the agent either incurred
the targeted waste, such as an unnecessary reinstall, or used the stated fact, such as a native test runner;
without a need detector, every exercised task counts as needed. These records enter Si and decide which
skills are activated or retired (examples in Appendix I).
Activation and retirement. A candidate is activated, entering ∆Pi , only while two conditions hold. It must
have been confirmed on several tasks whose edited file sets do not overlap, so that its evidence reflects
repository-level rather than issue-local recurrence. Its need rate, the fraction of exercising trajectories that
needed it, must also stay at or above a threshold. The first condition asks whether a skill is reliable, the
second whether repeatedly presenting it is worthwhile. The support of f at task i is

3.2 Meta-strategy II: Tool Pruning
Tool pruning reduces the recurring cost of the agent’s tool interface by omitting schemas unlikely to help with
the current task. The controller always retains core tools for shell execution and file reading, writing, and
editing. Other tools are removed after a fixed number of consecutive completed tasks have left them uncalled.
In addition, an LLM judge assesses the relevance of each never-called tool not yet removed, using the tool’s
description and the description of τi , in which links to the repository’s pull requests, issues, commits, and files
are masked. A tool is removed when it meets the unused-history criterion or the judge deems it irrelevant;
the remaining tools form Ui and are loaded for task τi . To recover from mistaken exclusions, the controller
periodically restores the full tool catalog for a re-audit. A tool called during a re-audit is never masked again,
since only never-called tools are masked.

3.3 Meta-strategy III: Failure-loop Intervention
The controller also derives a repository-specific turn budget from prior trajectories. Let |ξ j | denote the number
of turns in trajectory ξ j , and let y j ∈ {0, 1} indicate whether task τj succeeded. Let Q p denote the empirical
p-th percentile of the turn counts of successful prior tasks. The budget for τi is

Bi = β · Q p {|ξ j | : j < i, y j = 1} ,

(3)

with p high and a margin β > 1. Here y j comes from the benchmark grader, but in realistic settings any
comparable success signal can be used. The budget is a reference for identifying unusually long trajectories,
not by itself a stopping point. After each tool call, a rule-based check looks for repeated failure signatures,
dense error output, or a trajectory that has exceeded Bi , without making a model call. When triggered, an
LLM judge reviews a compact digest of recent actions and running counters, including commands, edited file
names, and error signatures, to distinguish an unproductive loop from a difficult task that is still progressing.
A confident stuck verdict first redirects the agent with a summary of its recent actions and a request to try
a different approach. If the failure pattern recurs and the judge again finds the agent stuck, the controller
terminates the rollout. Productive or uncertain verdicts let it continue past Bi , up to the forced stop below.

4 Experiments
We compare TokenEconomizer with default execution, reduced reasoning effort, and fixed strategies on cost
and task success across repositories and agent–model settings, then test transfer to web development and
security auditing.

4.1 Setup
Agents, benchmarks, and methods. We evaluate five agent–model settings: Claude Code with Sonnet 5
and with Opus 4.8, Copilot CLI with Sonnet 5, and Codex with GPT-5.6 Terra and GPT-5.6 Sol. Every setting
runs the same 277 SWE-Bench tasks from 6 repositories (Jimenez et al., 2024): scikit-learn (60), SymPy
(60), Django (40), Sphinx (44), Matplotlib (34), and xarray (39). These tasks span both the verified split
and harder tasks from the full split. Within a repository, tasks run in chronological order, so the controller
only learns from work that came earlier in that repository’s history. Three additional benchmarks assess
generalizability to other domains (Sec. 4.3): 102 sequential Web-Bench tasks from 5 projects (Xu et al., 2025),
and 100 security tasks each from CredData (credential detection) and ReposVul (vulnerability detection)
(Wang et al., 2024a). Appendix D describes how we selected the tasks and streams for each benchmark. In
every setting, default execution is the unmodified agent at high reasoning effort, with no external control.
We compare it against agent-native execution at medium and low effort, the two fixed strategies RTK and
RepoMap, TokenEconomizer, and TokenEconomizer combined with RTK or RepoMap. Serena is left out
because tool pruning could remove its unused server and so confound the comparison (Appendix B); it was
also the only fixed strategy that raised pooled cost in cost profiling (Fig. 2).
Evaluation metrics. Our quality metric is Pass@1 under the official grader on SWE-Bench, cumulative Pass@2
on Web-Bench, as recommended by the original benchmark, and detection F1 on the security benchmarks
(more details are included in Appendix D); efficiency is mean cache-aware cost (Appendix E, Eq. (4)),
which charges every auxiliary controller call to the task that triggered it. Appendix C lists the controller
configuration shared by all benchmarks.

4.2 SWE-Bench Results
Table 1 shows that TokenEconomizer alone reduces mean cache-aware cost by 18.4–30.9% across all five
settings. Pass@1 improves in four settings and only falls by 1.0 percentage point on Opus 4.8. Lowered
reasoning effort instead reduces Pass@1 by 1.8–10.3 percentage points, and RTK or RepoMap alone saves
inconsistently. TokenEconomizer can also be combined with either: both combinations reduce cost in all five
settings, and on Claude Code with Opus 4.8, adding RTK raises the saving from 30.9% to 33.2% with Pass@1
above default.
The savings are consistent. Pooled savings can conceal cost increases in individual environments, as the
fixed strategies in Sec. 2 illustrate. Fig. 5a reports cost changes for each of the six repositories under each of
the five agent–model settings, yielding 30 repository–setting cells. TokenEconomizer reduces cost in all 30,
by 5.2–40.1%, as does TokenEconomizer+RTK. TokenEconomizer+RepoMap reduces cost in 29 of 30. By
comparison, RTK alone increases cost in 12 cells and RepoMap alone in 15.
Adaptation expands the Pareto frontier. Fig. 1b places each method’s cost and Pass@1 change relative
to default execution in its agent–model setting. Reduced reasoning effort achieves large savings with
substantial losses in Pass@1, while RTK and RepoMap stay closer to default and move in mixed directions.

4.3 Generalization to Diverse Task Structures
To assess generalization beyond SWE-Bench, we evaluate TokenEconomizer on sequential web development
(Web-Bench) and two security-auditing benchmarks (CredData and ReposVul). Web-Bench typically involves
smaller codebases and workspaces than SWE-Bench, but its tasks remain within one continuous project
and naturally inherit earlier workspace changes. In the security benchmarks, each task targets a different
repository under a fixed audit prompt, so the procedure recurs without repository continuity. Because no
repository recurs, we disable repository-specific micro-skill injection, leaving only two meta-strategies active.
Table 2b shows cost reductions of 16.0–21.2% on Web-Bench and 16.5–19.6% on CredData. The latter result
shows that the remaining meta-strategies can still achieve substantial savings across new repositories under
a shared audit procedure. Reductions are much smaller on ReposVul, at 6.4–9.3%, where shorter trajectories
leave less removable cost. The savings thus generalize across these workloads, while their magnitude varies
with the reusable structure and cost-reduction headroom within the trajectories.

5 Related Work
Cost reduction for LLMs and coding agents. A classic line of work lowers inference cost by choosing which
model answers each query: cascades escalate from cheaper to stronger models until an answer is judged
reliable (Chen et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2024; Aggarwal et al., 2024), and routers predict the cheapest adequate
model before generation (Ding et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2024). These methods trade answer quality for cost on
each query, whereas TokenEconomizer keeps the model and effort fixed and removes cost that recurs within
an environment. For coding agents, existing methods reduce cost within a trajectory by controlling context,
interfaces, or rollout length. Context-control methods compress prompts (Jiang et al., 2023), shell output
with RTK, tool observations (Kovács, 2026), or accumulated trajectories (Xiao et al., 2025); others narrow
retrieval through repository maps, knowledge graphs (Vogel et al., 2026), structure-aware scouting (Li
et al., 2026b), or trained retrieval agents (Bashir et al., 2026). Interface-level methods restrict or retrieve
candidate tools (Paramanayakam et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024), enrich tool interfaces (Liu et al., 2025), or
encourage minimal implementations through plugins such as Ponytail. Runtime methods provide corrective
feedback (Zhu et al., 2025), early-exit checks (Lu et al., 2025), budget estimates (Lin et al., 2026), or hiddenstate failure detection (Ruan et al., 2026). Methods deployable through an agent’s exposed interfaces are
typically fixed and task-local; our cost profiling (Sec. 2) finds that their effects vary across repositories.
TokenEconomizer instead uses completed traces to adapt the instruction prefix, tool set, and stopping policy
to each environment.
9

Cross-task experience for coding agents. Recent benchmarks have started to evaluate agents across continual
repair, related-task context reuse, and streaming task sequences (Joshi et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026; Yan
et al., 2026). Prior methods reuse completed executions as verbal lessons or workflows (Shinn et al., 2023;
Zhao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024c), or derive repository knowledge from commit histories and repair
trajectories (Wang et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2025). A complementary line of work packages experience
into reusable capabilities, including executable skill and tool libraries (Wang et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024;
Yuan et al., 2024), verified programmatic skills (Wang et al., 2025b), refined skill APIs (Zheng et al., 2025),
and evolving test-time libraries (Xu et al., 2026). Recent systems further automate skill construction and
evolution (Wang et al., 2026b; Ni et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a).
Because indiscriminate repository context can increase cost without generally improving success (Gloaguen
et al., 2026), such experience must be reused selectively. Building on this principle, TokenEconomizer uses
cross-task experience to learn cost-control policies applied through agents’ exposed interfaces.

6 Limitations and Conclusion
Limitations. Each meta-strategy can still make an incorrect decision: a micro-skill may become stale, a
pruned tool may become necessary, and a difficult but productive task may be mistaken for a failure loop.
Each decision, however, comes with a recovery path that limits the damage: a stale skill is retired once a trace
contradicts it, a wrongly pruned tool returns at the next re-audit, and the failure-loop intervention redirects
before it stops and spares uncertain cases. Our experiments cover three widely used coding agents but not
more minimal harnesses such as Pi; since TokenEconomizer relies only on the prompt, tool configuration,
and event stream, supporting such a harness requires an adapter rather than new meta-strategies. Finally,
our SWE-Bench streams consist of related but independent fixes rather than actual user sessions. Continuous
sessions should offer more reusable experience (Appendix A), so interactive coding workflows are a natural
next test.
Conclusion. Coding-agent costs accumulate across tasks in recurring environments, which isolated-task
evaluations overlook. TokenEconomizer shows that an external controller can learn from completed work to
make an unchanged agent cheaper while largely preserving task success, across software engineering, web
development, and security auditing.

A Extended Discussion
TokenEconomizer as one instance of a broader class. TokenEconomizer occupies one point in a larger
design space: external controllers that apply fixed, environment-agnostic meta-strategies and let completed
traces set environment-specific policies. Each of our meta-strategies follows the same pattern, pairing a cheap
signal from traces with a conservative action and a way to recover from mistakes: micro-skills are confirmed
by detectors and retired on contradiction, pruned tools return through re-audits, and the failure-loop guard
redirects before it stops. The same pattern applies to other controls that harnesses expose, such as choosing
the reasoning effort or model for each task, compacting context at learned points, or reusing environment
setup across tasks, and each strategy could be specialized further to a deployment, for example with longer
histories, micro-skills shared across related repositories, or success signals from users rather than benchmark
graders. Because the controller needs no model access, retraining, or change to the agent, it can follow agents
as they evolve. We therefore see our results as evidence that this direction is promising and underexplored,
not as its ceiling.
Generalization without per-environment tuning. A single configuration (Table 4) serves three harnesses,
five agent–model settings, and four benchmarks; the only benchmark-specific changes follow from the
structure of the streams (Appendix C). That the savings hold in all 30 SWE-Bench repository–setting cells
without tuning suggests that the meta-strategies capture regularities of agentic work, namely recurring
operational facts, unused interfaces, and unproductive loops, rather than artifacts of one agent or dataset.
The shared configuration is a default rather than a constraint, and its parameters also serve as knobs with a
predictable direction: fewer required confirmations or a lower need gate activate micro-skills sooner, a shorter
unused streak or a longer re-audit period keeps more tools pruned, and a smaller budget margin β, a lower
judge confidence threshold, or a smaller κ lets the guard intervene earlier. Each adjustment trades additional
savings against a higher risk of a wrong decision, so users can move along the cost–quality frontier to suit
their priorities, much as with reasoning effort, whose savings come with larger losses in Pass@1 (Fig. 1b).
SWE-Bench is also a conservative test of experience reuse. Consecutive tasks are independent: each starts
from a fresh checkout of its own base commit, none inherits the agent’s earlier edits, and each stream spans
1.9 to 8.5 years of its repository’s history, over which layouts, test commands, and dependencies drift. A
developer’s session instead returns to a codebase that changes incrementally, often through the agent’s own
work, so more of what one task teaches should remain valid for the next. Web-Bench, whose tasks inherit the
workspace, is closer to that setting, and TokenEconomizer saves 16.0–21.2% there even though its streams
have only 20 or 21 tasks.
13

Complementarity with fixed strategies. Alone, RTK and RepoMap change Pass@1 inconsistently, by −3.8
to +1.9 points across settings (Table 1). Their combinations with TokenEconomizer are more informative.
On Claude Code with Opus 4.8, where TokenEconomizer alone is 1.0 point below default, adding RepoMap
or RTK raises Pass@1 to 75.1 and 74.8, above default, and a combination attains the highest Pass@1 in three
of the five settings. A plausible explanation is that the strategies supply different information. Micro-skills
state how to operate in a repository, such as how to run its tests, but by design say nothing about where a fix
belongs; RepoMap’s ranked signatures address that localization question, and RTK’s condensed logs keep
failure messages salient in a shorter context. The savings, however, do not simply add up: RepoMap bills its
map on every turn, so TokenEconomizer+RepoMap saves less than TokenEconomizer alone in four of five
settings, and RTK, whose own savings are inconsistent, adds to TokenEconomizer’s saving on Opus 4.8 but
lowers it by up to 5.2 points elsewhere. Combining fixed and adaptive strategies thus trades some savings for
quality, and which combination is preferable depends on the setting.
Why micro-skills. We call the injected knowledge micro-skills to distinguish it from two forms of reusable
experience in prior work (Sec. 5). Skill and tool libraries package executable code, programs, or APIs
for recurring subtasks, while lessons, workflows, and repository memories distill past trajectories into
natural-language guidance on how to solve tasks. A micro-skill is narrower than either: a single operational
fact about one environment, such as which test runner works, where sources live, or which setup step is
unnecessary. Three properties make this granularity suitable for cost control. First, it targets the cost we
observe: frontier agents already solve most tasks, and much of their removable spend goes to rediscovering
the same environment facts from task to task (Sec. 2) rather than to missing problem-solving ability. Second,
it is continually verifiable: detectors confirm or contradict a one-line claim about commands and outputs on
every later trace without a model call, so its validity is rechecked throughout the stream rather than once
when it is created, whereas whether a general lesson or procedure helped is hard to read from a trace. Third,
it is cheap and low-risk: the manual is billed on every turn, so each entry must be short and earn its place,
and because a micro-skill states facts rather than solutions, it carries no task-specific content from earlier
tasks into later ones, and a wrong one is retired after little evidence, as Example (c) in Appendix I shows.

External cost-saving strategies.
• RTK (v0.45.0) is registered as a pre-execution hook on the agent’s shell tool. It rewrites each shell command
to run through RTK, which condenses the command’s output, such as test logs, listings, and diffs, before

14

• Serena is an MCP server backed by language servers. It adds symbol-level navigation tools, such as finding
a symbol’s definition, listing a file’s symbols, and finding references. The agent is instructed to prefer
these tools over its native file reading and search, which remain available.
• RepoMap refers to Aider’s repository map. It parses the repository’s Python source, ranks files and symbols
by their centrality in the cross-reference graph, biased toward identifiers mentioned in the task description,
and renders the top-ranked signatures within a 2,048-token budget. The map is generated once per task
and supplied as project instructions, so it becomes part of the prefix billed on every turn.
The main experiments use the same RTK version and map budget, and also test TokenEconomizer+RTK and
TokenEconomizer+RepoMap to determine whether adaptive savings complement output compression and
static repository context. There is no TokenEconomizer+Serena variant, because tool pruning may remove
an unused Serena server and so directly confound the two methods; for the same reason, Serena is absent
from Table 1.
Headroom measurements. All three measurements read the default-execution traces and estimate exposure
to removable cost, which should be interpreted as an upper bound rather than achievable savings.
• Repeated actions (Fig. 3a). We reduce each tool call to an action key. For shell commands, the key is the
command lower-cased, with whitespace collapsed, any leading directory change removed, and only the
first 6 tokens kept. For file tools, the key is the tool name and the file’s base name. Within each repository,
tasks are taken in chronological order, and for each task we measure the share of its tool calls whose key
already appeared in an earlier task of the same repository. The cross-task control measures the same
share against the complete history of each other repository, averaged over the five.
• Unused tools (Fig. 3b). We compare the tool catalog the agent receives at session start, which has 23 tools
and is the same in every repository, with the tool calls it actually makes, counted per repository.
• Failed tasks (Fig. 3c). We measure the share of default spend that goes to tasks that fail, and the mean turn
count of passed and failed tasks. For the spend after a trajectory first repeats a failure signature, each
run’s cost is spread evenly over its tool calls, which understates late spend, since every turn re-bills the
context accumulated before it.

a benchmark, every repository and project uses the same values, and none is tuned per repository, project, or
stream. All values were fixed before the main runs, in earlier development runs on the same six SWE-Bench
repositories. These values serve six SWE-Bench repositories whose streams range from 34 to 60 tasks and
whose default pass rates, turn counts, and tool usage differ widely, as well as five Web-Bench projects that
span Web standards and frameworks.
The settings differ across benchmarks only where the structure of the stream requires it. Web-Bench streams
have 20 or 21 tasks, too few for the percentile of passed turn counts to warm up before most of the stream
has run. The failure-loop guard therefore starts from a pre-registered budget of 30 turns until 5 tasks have
passed. On the security benchmarks every task is a different repository, so a repository-specific micro-skill
never has a later task in the same repository to apply to; micro-skill injection is disabled. Their tasks are also
read-only audits under one fixed brief, so the unused-tool history reflects the audit procedure rather than
any single repository, and the judge assesses each candidate against that brief.

SWE-Bench. We use the six repositories profiled in Sec. 2, all among the largest in SWE-Bench Verified. Of

tasks are typically days apart (median 4 days between issue dates).
We draw the extra scikit-learn and SymPy tasks from the full split to raise the difficulty of the two streams.
With Verified tasks alone, nearly every method passes nearly every task in these repositories, so default
execution cannot be told apart from its reduced-effort variants. The added tasks have gold patches of 61–200
changed lines and a package version that already appears in the stream; we sort candidates by patch size
and sample at even spacing. Because the full split was not validated by human annotators, we checked each
added task by applying its gold patch in the official image and running the official evaluation, and every one
passed. Each repository’s stream runs in base-commit order. Because fix commits are public, every method
runs behind an answer-key guard that blocks direct access to the gold patch on GitHub (Appendix J).
Web-Bench. Web-Bench consists of 50 projects, each a sequence of 20 dependent tasks, and some projects
open with an extra init task that sets up the scaffold. We run five projects as streams in the benchmark’s
own task order: calculator (21 tasks), table (21), expressjs (20), react (20), and zustand (20), for 102 tasks in
total. Each setting runs every stream with three seeds. We chose the five for diversity across the benchmark’s
two halves, Web standards and Web frameworks. Calculator and table are built on plain HTML, CSS, and
JavaScript, the latter around the HTML <table> element. React is a front-end UI framework, zustand a
state-management library used with React, and expressjs a Node.js back end with a database. We also
excluded projects on which default execution fails early: the official protocol stops a stream at the first task
that fails both attempts, so such a project would leave too short a stream to compare methods on.
16

We follow the official Web-Bench evaluation protocol (Xu et al., 2025), which allows each task two attempts.
If the first attempt fails the project’s tests, the agent retries the same task with the test errors as context. The
retry matters because each task builds on the previous one: without it, a single failed attempt would end the
stream and leave every later task unevaluated. We therefore report Pass@2, the fraction of tasks passed on
either attempt, and our cost figures include both attempts. As on SWE-Bench, every method runs behind the
answer-key guard (Appendix J), because the grading specifications are public.
CredData. CredData contains 333 repositories. We exclude the 19 that contain no true secret line: for those,
predicting nothing scores an F1 of 1, so they measure only precision. We split the remaining 314 into quartiles
by the number of true secret lines per repository, which is heavily skewed (cut points 4, 8.5, and 24.75),
allocate the 100 slots in proportion (28, 22, 25, and 25), and select that many repositories from each quartile.
The selected repositories contain between 1 and 799 true secret lines (median 8.5).
ReposVul. From the 1,310 instances of the Python split, we drop the 298 whose fix removes no line and so
leaves nothing to detect, and the 109 that ReposVul flags as outdated. From the remaining 903 we sample 100
as for CredData, by quartile of vulnerable-line count (cut points 2, 5, and 17; 31, 21, 24, and 24 slots) with a
fixed seed. The selected instances have 1 to 411 vulnerable lines (median 5). As on CredData, every task
is a different repository under the same fixed audit prompt, and each seed runs all 100. We grade strictly
at the line level: a reported location counts only if its file and line match a line the fixing commit removed,
and precision is taken over reported lines in the vulnerable files. Prior work instead reports binary F1 over
whole functions or code samples on balanced sets (Lekssays et al., 2025); under our metric, naming the right
function but a different line scores zero. We keep the strict metric because it mirrors CredData’s line-level F1
and needs no further labels.

F Additional Meta-strategy Details
Micro-skill injection. The proposer is called after task 4 and every 5 tasks thereafter. Each call sees digests
of the repository’s 8 most recent tasks, which contain no solution text or gold patches, and returns at most 4
candidates. A candidate is discarded unless it cites at least 2 tasks in the window on which its confirming
detector matches. An admitted candidate’s detectors are run retroactively over the window’s tasks, so it
enters with their evidence and can be activated at once. On an exercised task, contradiction is checked before
confirmation, and a skill without a need detector counts every exercised task as needed. A skill the proposer
marks as high-stakes, such as a test command or a build step, retires at its first contradiction once it has met
the confirmation condition. Table 5 summarizes the lifecycle. The three criteria act at different points: the
confirmation count moves a candidate to confirmed, the need rate decides on each task whether a confirmed
skill is injected, and the support qi ( f ) decides retirement. Support is checked only once a skill is confirmed,
so a candidate with negative support, which is never injected, stays a candidate; if it is later confirmed, it
retires after the next task unless its support has turned positive.

Tool pruning. A tool’s unused streak resets whenever it is called. Before each task, unprotected, never-called
tools whose streak has reached three are masked; the other unprotected, never-called tools are sent to the
judge, and those it marks irrelevant are masked too; if the judge call fails, they stay loaded. The mask is fixed
for the whole task. A re-audit loads the full catalog without calling the judge.
Failure-loop intervention. The rule-based trigger keeps running counters of repeated commands and errors,
edit churn, distinct edited files, absence of state change, and repeated verification failures, which the judge’s
digest also reports. The judge is consulted only while the trigger is open, at most once every 5 turns; after
each productive verdict, this interval grows by 5 turns, up to 20. A forced stop is checked before the judge
and cannot be vetoed by it: once the trajectory’s turns, counted across a redirect, reach κBi with κ = 2.5, and
the strict stall rule holds, the rollout ends without a further redirect. A failure signature is the first error line
of a failing tool output, such as a traceback, a failed test, or an assertion, with numbers, memory addresses,
and file paths removed, so that two outputs that fail the same way match. The strict stall rule holds when
three conditions hold at once: edit churn, with at least 4 edits to one file or edits to at least 3 files; dense
18

error output, with at least 6 error outputs; and repetition, with at least 20% of failing outputs repeating an
earlier failure signature. The budget in Eq. (3) uses a high percentile of passed turn counts rather than the
maximum, so one unusually long success cannot inflate it, while repositories whose successful runs are
typically long still receive a proportionally larger budget. The redirect is an LLM-authored summary of the
agent’s own actions that asks it to try a materially different hypothesis.
